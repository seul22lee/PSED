## Atomic layer deposition on polymer fibers and fabrics for multifunctional and electronic textiles

a)

Alexandra H. Brozena, Christopher J. Oldham, and Gregory N. Parsons Department of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, North Carolina 27695-7905

(Received 8 October 2015; accepted 7 December 2015; published 28 December 2015)

Textile materials, including woven cotton, polymer knit fabrics, and synthetic nonwoven fiber mats, are being explored as low-cost, flexible, and light-weight platforms for wearable electronic sensing, communication, energy generation, and storage. The natural porosity and high surface area in textiles is also useful for new applications in environmental protection, chemical decontamination, pharmaceutical and chemical manufacturing, catalytic support, tissue regeneration, and others. These applications raise opportunities for new chemistries, chemical processes, biological coupling, and nanodevice systems that can readily combine with textile manufacturing to create new 'multifunctional' fabrics. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) has a unique ability to form highly uniform and conformal thin films at low processing temperature on nonuniform high aspect ratio surfaces. Recent research shows how ALD can coat, modify, and otherwise improve polymer fibers and textiles by incorporating new materials for viable electronic and other multifunctional capabilities. This article provides a current overview of the understanding of ALD coating and modification of textiles, including current capabilities and outstanding problems, with the goal of providing a starting point for further research and advances in this field. After a brief introduction to textile materials and current textile treatment methods, the authors discuss unique properties of ALD-coated textiles, followed by a review of recent electronic and multifunctional textiles that use ALD coatings either as direct functional components or as critical nucleation layers for active materials integration. The article concludes with possible future directions for ALD on textiles, including the challenges in materials, manufacturing, and manufacturing integration that must be overcome for ALD to reach its full potential in electronic and other emerging multifunctional textile systems. ## I. INTRODUCTION

Textiles and fibers make up many of our most commonly used products. Garments provide protection, warmth, visual appeal, identification, and other benefits. Fibrous materials also appear as furnishings, wall and floor coverings, hygiene and healthcare materials, filters, cable supports, and other basic infrastructure. Textiles and fibers appear in these applications because they are renewable and low cost materials, light weight and flexible, and feature well developed manufacturing. The high surface area of textiles also provides a large active region for interaction with the environment or other target areas, including the human body. For these reasons, textiles provide a convenient and common platform to add new functionality to current products.

Often, textiles are chosen to provide more than one function in a given application. Natural wool, for example, provides physical protection and repels water while retaining its thermal insulation under both wet and dry conditions. 'Multifunctional textiles' describe materials that are technologically modified to provide utility that is typically

Other names that describe multifunctional nonaesthetic textiles include 'technical textiles' and 'smart textile' systems. 2-6

One of the most well-known attributes of atomic layer deposition (ALD) is its ability to create films and coatings with excellent conformality across large areas with complex roughness and topography. Since fibers and textiles provide unique surface structures, many teams have studied ALD as a way to modify, encapsulate, mimic, or otherwise coat fibers and textiles to add new multifunctional capabilities. 35-40 Significant fundamental work has been done to understand the mechanisms in ALD modification of polymers 41,42 and other fiber materials, 43-47 and to identify and explore functionality that ALD can uniquely add to create practical textiles.

The aim of this article is to provide an overview of the current understanding and capability for ALD coating and modification of textiles, with specific attention for electronic and other emerging multifunctional textile systems. Section II provides a brief description of textile materials and processing, and discusses common research approaches for textile coating and modification, followed in Sec. III by a basic introduction to ALD on fibers including a discussion of materials and process challenges and limitations. Section IV then reviews the unique properties of ALD-coated textiles, and how ALD-based methods can modify and improve fibrous materials. In Sec. V, we show recent examples of electronic and multifunctional textiles that use ALD coatings either as functional components or as critical buildingblocks for active materials integration. Finally, Sec. VI presents possible future directions for ALD on textiles and describes the challenges that must be overcome for ALD to reach its full potential in this field.

## II. TEXTILE AND NONWOVEN MATERIALS FABRICATION AND MODIFICATION

## A. Textile materials fabrication

Textiles, including wovens, knits, and other forms of cloth, are made of both natural and synthetic materials that are spun, extruded, or otherwise formed into fibers and yarns. Nonwovens, including spun-bond, meltblown, and electrospun materials, 48-50 are typically comprised of small fibers with diameters of /C24 5-20 l m (or in the 100 nm range for electrospun nanofibers), 51,52 which are laid down on a moving platform to form continuous fiber mats. The physical arrangement and entanglement of fibers in nonwovens can be similar to felt wool or other natural mats. Nonwoven processing often makes use of recycled plastics and other goods to produce low-cost materials with positive environmental impact. The terms 'textile' and 'nonwoven' technically refer to different materials with distinct properties and function. However, in this article, the terms 'fabric,' textile, 'fiber mat,' and others will be used interchangeably with the understanding that the resulting function or use will likely differ.

## B. Textile surface modification

In textile processing, fibers, yarns, and fabrics may undergo many additional steps including dyeing, printing, and other chemical or physical 'finishing' treatments. 53,54 Finishing technology is often critical because it gives the material a desirable appearance or feel (i.e., by modifying fiber surface roughness), or a unique added function, such as

for UV protection or control of surface wetting, among other possibilities. Many of these finishing steps require heating for extended periods of time to dry the treated fabrics; so, these processes are typically energy intensive and expensive. Therefore, industry seeks new approaches that improve current function while minimizing energy use and environmental impact. As a surface modification method, ALD typically requires elevated temperatures as well, but heating times are generally shorter than traditional finishing treatments, which could result in overall lower energy use and greater economic savings.

Most common textile finishes are applied using solutionbased processing. For example, cotton is made wrinkle-free with a standard 'pad-dry-cure' process that uses an organic diamine crosslinker solution in the presence of an acid catalyst. 53 Other solution methods studied for fiber surface modification include sol-gel processing, dip-coating, electrostatic layer-by-layer deposition, spray coating, direct chemical grafting, and others. 2,55-63 Uniformity in solution-based processes is often acceptable, but not ideal. Environmental compatibility is another growing concern.

Vapor-phase processing is also used for surface finishing in textile manufacturing. Plasma surface modification can be done using inert or chemically active gases, and processes can be scaled to roll-to-roll at atmospheric pressure. 64,65 Acid groups, for example, can be attached to fibers via direct plasma excitation of the acid vapor, or they can be attached via solution processes after treatment by inert gas plasma to create functional radical sites on the fiber surface. 65 The plasma modification approach can work well to modify the surface-face of the textile, but modification is generally nonuniform throughout the fiber matrix. 66 Plasma modification requires excited species (e.g., radicals and/or neutral excited metastable atoms) to interact with the polymer, but these excited species readily degrade or recombine to their ground state upon collision with other gases or with the polymer surface. The degradation rate depends on the gas mean-freepath, the distance between polymer fibers, and the net fiber mat thickness. Therefore, getting radicals to penetrate beyond the near surface-face of the fiber mat is difficult, resulting in nonuniform modification of the fibers within the mat. For these same reasons, plasma modified ALD is limited in terms of uniform deposition of surface coatings on textiles and fiber mats. ALD and non-ALD treatments using other excited species that degrade upon collision, including ozone for example, 67 are also expected to have similar uniformity problems. Nevertheless, vapor modification by plasma or excited species can be a viable approach for many chemical treatments.

In addition to plasma techniques, several other vaporbased methods have been evaluated for textile treatment, including physical, chemical, and plasma vapor deposition; vapor chemical grafting; and vapor-sprayed electrolyte assemblies. 60,61,68-72 Early studies of fiber surface modification explored UV-promoted vapor chemical grafting. 72 Armstrong and Rutherford 72 showed that under UV irradiation, vinyl-containing acrylate, vinyl acetate, and styrene monomer vapors would readily react and bind to cellulosics, cellulose esters, polyamide, and polypropylene. They found that grafting a small amount of acrylonitrile onto cotton made it highly resistive to penicillium oxalicum, a cellulosedestroying fungus. They also found that grafting reactions were not limited to the fiber surface. UV grafting of polyacrylonitrile to acetate fibers increased the Young's modulus, which the authors ascribed to subsurface monomer diffusion and crosslinking. However, the UV grafting procedure lacked uniformity. Loosely wound yarns could be coated evenly, whereas uniformity was poor on fiber mats. 72

Direct chemical vapor deposition (CVD) typically requires high temperatures which are not suitable for many polymer fibers. However, using a vapor-phase oxidant or radical initiator, oxidative and initiated-CVD ( i -CVD) can produce polymer coatings at low temperature. 73 For example, Ma et al . used i -CVD of perfluoroalkyl methyl methacrylate with a tert -butyl peroxide initiator to create a superhydrophic coating on electrospun fiber mats. 68 In this process, the extent of polymerization was determined by the availability of the active initiator, and conformal polymer coatings were achieved when sufficient initiator penetrated through the fiber mat. In thicker mats, a profile of initiator surface concentration would be expected to lead to thicker polymer growth on top of the exposed mat surface.

New surface treatments are needed for future multifunctional or smart textiles, especially those that directly couple new electronic, sensing, chemical, security, and other functionalities. In addition, new surface modification strategies that modify surface wetting, add chemical stability, control thermal conductivity, allow selective adhesion, or adjust biological response may prove to be more important as a means to prepare fibers and textiles for subsequent processes that would otherwise not be viable on fibers. Such processes could promote new low-cost multifunctional fiber and fabric materials for high-surface area catalysis, large-area chemical remediation, rapid low-energy water purification, and other areas of need.

## III. ALD ON TEXTILES AND FIBERS

ALD proceeds using alternating vapor exposures under conditions that create a sequence of self-limiting surface chemisorption reactions. 74-76 The process is well known and widely used in large-scale semiconductor manufacturing, in part, because it can deposit inorganic thin films with high conformality and precise nanoscale thickness on complex high-aspect ratio geometries. The self-limiting nature of ALD surface reactions allows uniform coatings on nonplanar, porous, and otherwise tortuous substrates. Early work recognized that fiber substrates are a unique platform for ALD to create new three-dimensional inorganic lattices with chemical functionality (Fig. 2). 43,77

Under certain conditions, the transport of reactants can be very rapid in some polymer materials and negligible in others. Gong et al . 85 demonstrated selective reactant transport and reaction by treating bicomponent fibers with ALD. When fibers with a polylactic acid core and polypropylene sheath were exposed to short TMA/water dose steps at 120 /C14 C, TEM analysis showed a thin conformal metal oxide on the outer polypropylene surface. However, when longer exposures were used, TMA was found to diffuse through the polypropylene and selectively react with the central polylactic acid core region. Selective polymer modification via ALD could extend to other multicomponent materials to create new polymer/inorganic nanocomposite textiles. In subsequent experiments, the extent of subsurface diffusion was also found to depend on reactant purge time, where longer purge steps allowed more precursor to be transported out of the polymer bulk.

These examples show possible outcomes during ALD on polymers, but other processes could also occur, including chemical and structural modification of the polymer itself. It is important to note that even under conditions that lead to well-controlled ALD on solid surfaces, reactions can occur on or within polymers that are beyond those described as ALD.

## C. ALD conformality and uniformity within fiber mats

In principle, the self-limiting half-reactions in ALD allow a uniform and conformal coating to form on all surfaces exposed in the reactor deposition zone. However, full growth requires reactant saturation, which in turn requires each local surface to receive sufficient vapor exposure during each half cycle. Reactant purge steps between each half cycle must also be sufficient to remove byproducts and unused reactants.

Most studies of ALD on textiles use viscous-flow batch ALD reactors where the reactants flow over the fabric sample. In this configuration, reactant exposure time must be sufficient to allow species to diffuse into the regions between fibers. Likewise, purge cycles must be long enough to allow reaction products to desorb and diffuse out of the fiber structure and into the reaction flow stream. In this case, the meanfree path for gas phase species within the fiber mat can be close to the fiber-fiber distance, leading to relatively slow Knudsen or molecular flow conditions.

To modify reactant and byproduct transport in porous fibrous materials, Ritala et al . 77 demonstrated a 'flow-through' reactor design where the reactant species were convectively forced to flow between fibers within a filter paper substrate. The pores size allowed sufficient gas conductance to avoid a large pressure drop across the substrate. Hyde et al . 105 also explored the issue of flow geometry during ALD of Al2O3 on polypropylene [Fig. 4(a)] and found that orienting nonwoven polypropylene mats parallel or perpendicular to the gas-flow, i.e., 'flow over' or 'flow through' configurations, produced similar coatings under the conditions used.

Many studies 35,43,81,101,105-109 have confirmed the conformality of ALD coatings on fibers using electron microscopy analysis. Based on the analysis of the fiber materials and ALD process conditions in these reports, we find that when fiber mats with moderate density (i.e., open volume fraction of 50%-85%) 106 are coated under ALD conditions using moderate reactant exposure (e.g., /C24 1 s/cycle at 1-10 Torr), the fibers pulled from the fiber mats generally show good uniformity and excellent conformality. The overall surface area of the fibers can be quantified in terms of a 'surface area enhancement factor,' 106 or aspect ratio, 45,110,111 i.e., the total surface area of the fibers per unit projected surface area of the fiber mat. This ratio is effectively equivalent to the aspect ratio used for trenches, vias, and straight pores. Ideally, one would like to quantify coating uniformity through the fiber mat. In one report, infrared (IR) transmission spectroscopy was used to assess uniformity of ALD coatings within nonwoven mats by analyzing fiber sections. 105 Nonwoven mats are often made of multiple layers of overlaid fibers, and the layers can be mechanically separated or peeled from each other. Following Al2O3 ALD under typical moderate conditions, nonwoven fiber mats were physically peeled into thin layers and measured using IR spectroscopy to monitor the intensity of the Al-O stretching peak. Normalizing the intensity using characteristic IR peaks in the polymer, the Al-O intensity was found to be the same on each of the five layers extracted from a single nonwoven mat, demonstrating uniform coating throughout the mat using practical ALD conditions.

A more full understanding of ALD reactant transport and surface saturation within and through complex surface geometries can be approached through experiment and modeling. 45,105,106,112,113 Musschoot et al . studied the uniformity of ALD Al2O3 on nonwoven polyester fiber mats using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive xray analysis (EDX). 45 By allowing reactants to diffuse into the nonwoven mat on only one face, the authors measured the deposition penetration distance into various mats versus reactant exposure time and compared results to a random diffusion and reaction model. Under ALD conditions, a few seconds of reactant exposure per cycle produced a coating extending 5-10 mm into the fiber mat. The extent of coating and coating profile were consistent with model predictions. Furthermore, the penetration distance decreased significantly using plasma ALD. Specifically, while thermal Al2O3 ALD penetrated more than 10 mm into the mat, replacing water with plasma-excited oxygen led to less than 3 mm penetration, consistent with rapid decay of plasma exited species via collisions in the fiber network.

In another study, Sweet et al . used local compression of nonwovens to adjust and analyze diethyl zinc (DEZ) reactant penetration through fiber networks during ALD of ZnO. Growth profiles measured with the fiber mat agreed with model predictions, where a few seconds of DEZ exposure per cycle coated the fibers within 1-2 mm of the top mat face. 106 The deeper penetration observed by Musschoot et al . 45 was consistent with the larger void faction in their nonwovens ( /C24 95%) compared to those materials studied by Sweet et al . ## IV. PROPERTIES OFALD COATED TEXTILES AND FIBERS

## A. ALD for control of surface energy and wetting

The demand for self-cleaning and high-performance textiles provides ample motivation to study how ALD can

control surface wetting properties of fibers. The nature of water interaction and wetting on textured surfaces depends on both the fractional surface area in contact with the water and the surface energy of the material. Increasing the surface energy, such as through the deposition of polar metal oxide nanocoatings using ALD, is one way to increase the wetting capabilities of the substrate. 114-116 Upon coating by Al2O3 ALD, nonwetting hydrophobic polypropylene fibers can transition to either a metastable hydrophobic or a fully wetting hydrophilic state, due in part to the natural wettability of the polar Al2O3 surface. 105 On the other hand, when naturally wetting fabrics such as cotton are coated by ALD metal oxide, a few ALD cycles can cause a transition to a metastable hydrophobic state [Fig. 4(c)] and back to wetting depending on the number of ALD cycles [Fig. 4(d)]. 105,117 The transition between wetting and nonwetting occurs abruptly with the number of ALD cycles. Correlating the surface wetting characteristics of coated planar and textured fabric surfaces, the observed trends can be understood in terms of the well-known Cassie-Baxter and Wenzel models for wetting of roughened and textured surfaces. 118,119

The wetting transitions on polypropylene are found to proceed more readily when Al2O3 ALD is done at low temperature (e.g., 60 /C14 C), whereas coatings at higher temperature ( &gt; 90 /C14 C) often retained the hydrophobic state. Subsequent studies concluded that surface roughening caused by excess subsurface reactant penetration at higher temperatures likely helped the fibers to remain nonwetting. In contrast, the wetting transitions on cotton are less sensitive to deposition temperature. IR and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis suggests that the rapid hydrophobic transition occurs when TMA consumes hydroxyl groups exposed on the cotton cellulose surface to create hydrophobic Al(OC)3 surface groups. Some surface roughening was also observed, which may also promote the hydrophobic transition. The hydrophobic state is relatively stable in air, but it can degrade over time upon coating by adventitious carbon. Deposition greater than ten cycles eventually overwhelms these effects, resulting in a smoother, Al-OH terminated surface, which raises the surface energy of the treated cotton, causing it to become hydrophilic once more. Similar trends were observed for ZnO deposition as well. 117 Alternative coatings, such as TiN, can also increase the hydrophobicity of cotton fabric. 120 The change in surface wetting of fiber mats using an ALD coating can be an important way to enable subsequent wet processing.

## B. Conductive coatings on textiles

Conductive fibers are attractive for many smart textile applications, including embedded sensors, flexible antennas, and displays. 121 Depositing metal in a conformal manner and with controllable thickness is essential to give flexible, fibrous materials uniform conductivity that is comparable to the bulk coating material. Solution methods are generally used to add conductivity to fabrics, 2,55-63 but they often add unwanted weight, change the feel, or reduce fabric flexibility. Conductive carbon nanotubes, for example, can be added to create composite fibers, but conductivity is often less than 10 S cm /C0 1 . 122 Likewise, Kevlar fibers coated with single-walled carbon nanotubes has conductivity limited to 65 S cm /C0 1 . 123

While many metal ALD processes are known, most often these require reduction or combustion steps at 250 /C14 C or above. Most studies of conductive ALD coatings on textile fibers focus on lower temperature thermal or plasma ALD materials, including W, Cu, Al-doped ZnO, ITO, and Pt. 79,84,101,104,106,124-126 One challenge in metal ALD on fibers is to quantify the resulting conductivity of the deposited material. To address this issue, Jur et al . 79 described a modified four-point probe to quantify fiber coating conductivity. The conductive percolation between fibers was maximized by physical compression, and the conductivity was estimated using the volume and known density of the deposited thin film. 125 Using this characterization method, ALD W films deposited at 200 /C14 C on quartz fibers were found to have a conductivity of /C24 1150 S cm /C0 1 , consistent with that of other W thin films, but less than the bulk W conductivity. 79

## C. Mechanical modification via vapor infiltration and metalation

Although subsurface ALD reactions can occur, coalescence of the thin film into a continuous coating will ultimately block diffusion of the reactants into the fiber. However, there are several cases where precursor diffusion and subsurface reaction may be desired to modify and improve the bulk properties of the fiber. 47,127-130 Bulkinfiltration of reactants (typically ALD metal halides or metal organic precursors) occurs during long exposure times, sometimes followed by relatively short coreactant exposure. This results in metalation of the organic compound, modification of the polymer crystals, and/or crosslinking between neighboring chains, sometimes leading to significant changes in the mechanical properties of the fibers. For example, Gregorczyk et al . 128 observed that for a limited number of DEZ infusion cycles ( &lt; 4), the ultimate tensile stress of cellulose fibers nearly doubled in value while the elastic modulus increased by nearly 2.5 /C2 . The authors ascribed this change to Zn-O covalent crosslinkages between the cellulose units. They also saw that increased cycles of infusion or reaction with the more acidic TMA molecule caused the mechanical behavior to regress, possibly due to degradation of the polymer backbone (Fig. 5). Similarly, Seung-Mo et al . 47 observed that applying ALD coatings using DEZ, titanium isopropoxide, and TMA to spider silk increased the fiber toughness, where the extent of change increased with multiple doses of the precursor without water exposure.

## E. Fiber encapsulation and protection

Physical stabilization and protection is another area where ALD has shown value on textiles and fibers. For example, Roy et al . 46 found after depositing 45 nm of ALD Al2O3 onto carbon fibers, the oxidation temperature increased from 250 to 600 /C14 C, behaving more like a temperature-insensitive ceramic than an organic material. Another growing area of interest for surface modification of textiles is UV stabilization and protection. UV exposure degrades fiber mechanical performance and leads to visible color change. Hanson et al . 80 showed that ZnO ALD coatings on paper provided good stability against UV degradation. Moreover, the ZnO layer acted to adjust and controllably neutralize the acid content of archived paper. The good UV absorbance of many metal oxides makes ALD a viable route for UV protection, but new advances are needed to increase process speed and reduce cost.

In addition to protection from high energy sources, ALD coatings can protect textiles from mechanical forces relevant for personal protection. Kevlar V R , for example, provides excellent protection from blunt impact (i.e., ballistic trauma); however, it is susceptible to cutting or slashing by sharp objects. Therefore, when both ballistic and cut threats are present, personnel must wear multiple heavy protective garments. Combining blunt force and cut protection in one material could significantly reduce the weight of protective gear. Atanasov et al . 134 examined various types and combinations of ALD materials to address this need. Using Al2O3 ALD coatings from TMA/water at 50 or 100 /C14 C, spectroscopic and TEM analysis showed that TMA tended to react with the Kevlar V R and degrade its mechanical performance.

Inorganic ALD layers on polymer films can significantly reduce transmission of water and other vapors into and through the polymer. 135 However, metal oxide coatings are intrinsically stiff and they can easily crack or degrade when flexed, compromising water-resistance. One approach to make more mechanically robust protective films is to incorporate organic linking groups into the ALD sequence. For instance, organic molecules, such as glycidol or ethylene glycol, can be used in place of water as the oxidant in the reaction and alternately pulsed with ALD organometallic reagents to deposit inorganic-organic hybrid thin films in a method called, molecular layer deposition (MLD). 136 The resulting MLD processes can create fully organic polymer or oligomer films, or covalent metal oxide-organic network polymers, combining a variety of metals with a wide set of possible organic reactants that can be conformally deposited on high surface area materials like electrospun polyvinyl alcohol and nylon-6 nanofibers. 116,136,137 These 'metalcone' MLD thin films can also be combined with ALD layers to improve overall mechanical performance of oxygen or water barrier coatings on planar polymer substrates. 138,139

Nanofibers are attractive for biological or chemical filtration applications, but their large surface-to-volume ratio makes them inherently unstable under exposure to organic solvents. Various surface modification and treatments, including ALD, have been used to add functionality or improve stability. 37,116,140,141 However, while some nanofibers can be readily encased by ALD metal oxides, the ALD process can also damage or chemically degrade the nanofiber. The amide bonds in electrospun nylon nanofibers, for example, are highly reactive with TMA. 116 The challenge of improving chemical stability of nanofibers while maintaining mechanical flexibility was addressed by Oldham et al . 116 using a combination of ALD and MLD. The authors found that a thin ALD layer of ZnO could encase the fiber, allowing a mechanically flexible MLD layer to be subsequently deposited using TMA/glycidol. The bilayer coating enables the fiber mat to retain its nanofiber structure under chemical exposure, while maintaining sufficient flexibility to perform well during load-bearing air filtration (Fig. 7).

## F. ALD surface priming for subsequent reaction

ALD coatings can be added to fibers to form a reactive base layer, thereby enabling subsequent functionality to be uniformly added throughout the textile material. Peng et al . 142 showed the basic premise of ALD-based surface chemical activation of inert polymer fibers. Using an ALD metal oxide coating on polypropylene, the authors created a high density of well-defined reactive sites (metal hydroxide groups) that allowed subsequent chemical functionalization via vapor phase grafting of aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES). After immersing the APTES functionalized polypropylene in Au colloidal (40 nm) solution for 24 h, the fiber matrix took on a deep red color, due to electrostatic nanoparticle interactions with the available amine sites on the APTES. The authors also noted that the uniformity and chemical versatility of vapor-phase ALD provides advantages over other methods 143 for activating fiber surfaces.

In addition to organic monolayer functionalization, ALD on fibers can be useful to create well defined nucleation layers for subsequent hydrothermal 144-146 or layer-by-layer synthesis. 147 ALD ZnO on polymer fibers seeds lowtemperature hydrothermal growth of ZnO nanorods, creating high surface area photocatalytic fibers. 144,145 Recent results also show that ALD layers can nucleate high-quality metal organic framework crystals, 146,147 leading to new options for chemically absorptive and catalytic fibers and textiles, as discussed in more detail in Sec. V.

## V. EXAMPLES OF MULTIFUNCTIONAL AND ELECTRONIC TEXTILES ENABLED BYALD

The self-limiting reactions during low-temperature ALD provides a unique ability to create uniform and conformal coatings and surface modification on fibers. Moreover, under typical laboratory ALD conditions, coating uniformity can extend throughout moderately dense fiber mats and textile fabrics. In this section, we review recent examples where ALD is used to directly add electronic or multifunctional performance to textiles.

## A. Electronic conduction, capacitance, and sensing

Reliable and uniform conductivity is a primary need for electronic device integration into textiles. Several examples have shown how conductive ALD coatings can be used to form functional electronic devices on a textile platform. Jur et al ., 79 for example, created a functional liquid chemical flow-through capacitive sensor using fiber electrodes coated with ALD W. Two pieces of W-coated material were sewn together with an intervening layer of polypropylene to form a simple metal-insulator-metal capacitor. As an all-fabric sensor, the device showed good sensitivity to different liquid chemicals (Fig. 8). In this case, the nonwoven insulator within the capacitor served as a passive sponge to collect the liquid being sensed. In future systems, material coatings that allow selective chemical adsorption or capture could be used to create wearable or otherwise widely deployable capacitive sensing elements that can electronically detect and react to specific chemical species.

Feature patterning is critical for electronic integration. Most wiring in electronic textiles uses metal lines that are sprayed, glued, stamped, printed, or otherwise secured onto one side of the support fabric, or full circuits that are secured to fabrics by heat-sealing the stitching. This works well for topically applied circuits, but true electronic textiles will build circuits within the textile, and may require patterned materials to extend throughout the fiber mat. For ALD on planar surfaces, 'shadow' masking to create patterns does not work well because ALD reactants penetrate and deposit film in the thin gap region between the mask and the substrate. On fibrous substrates, precursor penetration under physical masks will be even more pronounced. However, if the fiber substrate is mechanically compressed by the mask, then lateral precursor transport can be slowed, creating uniform patterned coatings in the surface-exposed regions of the substrate. To demonstrate this, Sweet et al . 106 used physical masks to locally compress nonwoven and woven fabrics and study the extent of precursor transport into the compressed region during ALD of aluminum-doped ZnO. The random fiber network in the nonwoven helped impede lateral reactant diffusion and create more abrupt features, whereas the compressed woven fabric retained pathways for rapid lateral transport, leading to more diffuse patterns. A downside to this method is that the mask must be robust enough to apply mechanical force, so the minimum feature size will be limited. ## D. Chemically absorptive and reactive textiles

Metal-organic-frameworks (MOFs) are a novel class of materials that utilize metal-organic ligand interactions to generate microand nanoscale sized crystals with tunable pore sizes that result in record high porosity values. Because of the high porosity and surface area, MOFs have been proposed for filtration, separation, sensing, and gas storage applications. 164 However, these uses have been slow to develop, in part, due to the limitations of MOF processing in the powder phase.

Zhao et al . show that ALD coatings on polymer fibers can help promote nucleation of covalently bound HKUST-1 MOF crystals, producing a multifunctional MOF/fiber composite fabric by hydrothermal and layer-by-layer synthesis. 117,146 Using hydrothermal synthesis at 120 /C14 C, polypropylene nonwoven microfibers coated with ALD Al2O3 showed MOF mass uptake &gt; 50% larger than that on untreated fibers (Fig. 13). 146 Moreover, the ALD coating promoted heterogeneous MOF nucleation, whereas uncoated fibers led to more homogeneous nucleation, leading to nonattached MOF crystals between the fibers. The MOF/fiber composites showed an average surface area close to 700 m 2 /g, corresponding to &gt; 900 m 2 /g of MOF, consistent with that measured on similarly prepared crystal powders. The resulting mats showed NH3 dynamic loading &gt; 5mol/kgMOF þ fiber , exceeding previous reports for absorptive MOFs on fibers. Other fiber materials including PET and cellulose cotton also showed MOF uptake, although the MOF growth on cotton was less than on the other materials. The authors hypothesized that the Al2O3 on cotton promoted MOF nucleation, with poorer adhesion on the smooth cotton fiber surface. ALD ZnO and TiO2 also showed good results with a generally larger HKUST-1 mass uptake on the ZnO relative to Al2O3 and TiO2. ## VI. OUTLOOK AND CHALLENGES FOR ALD ON TEXTILES

Fiber and textile materials found throughout daily life are emerging as a versatile platform to introduce electronic or other nonaesthetic multifunctional capabilities that are easy to use and blend in with current clothing trends. As shown above, ALD is a promising technology to modify, improve, or otherwise add new performance capability to textiles. The promise of ALD for textiles stems from how the coating deposits on fibers and fabrics. Using controlled self-limiting ALD nucleation and growth conditions, textile materials are coated with a uniform thin shell of inorganic or organic material that leaves the weight, feel, and look generally unchanged. Other ALD-based processes may also be important to modify the near-surface or bulk materials of fibrous polymers.

The most demanding challenge for ALD in textiles is that common batch processing is too slow and costly for most applications. Therefore, early adoption of ALD will likely occur in high-value products such as specialty medical materials, high-performance aerospace composites, or advanced military applications where current fiber treatment processes are not sufficiently uniform or consistent to meet the product needs.

To fully realize the potential for ALD on textiles, new high-throughput processing methods are needed. The speed and cost required for ALD on textiles will depend on the industry and product target. Textile finishes for woven garments often run at line speeds of 10-20 m/min, which is close to many current spatial ALD deposition speeds. On the other hand, nonwovens are becoming a larger part of the textile industry because of low cost and high throughput, often exceeding 100 m/min. Continued advances in spatial and rollto-roll ALD, 165-168 flow-through coating processes, and the extension of spatial ALD to new materials will be important. The combination of high-surface area, porosity, and tortuosity found in fiber-networks will require better understanding of reactant transport and reaction on fibers, kinetics of byproduct desorption and diffusion, and improved reactor designs for ambient pressure operation (Fig. 14). Textile manufacturing is one of the oldest product industries, and many barriers will impede new technology adoption. Integrating a process like ALD into manufacturing plants with uncontrolled humidity, dust, and other contaminants will be challenging.

After surmounting these issues, early applications for lower-cost ALD on fibers will likely augment or improve current textile surface-treatments for chemical functionalization. Surface finishes on nonreactive polypropylene often rely on corona discharge or plasmas to attach temporary

bonding sites to one side of the fabric. For current solutionbased methods, surface modification by ALD could reduce chemical use and remove energy-intensive drying steps. ALD and MLD processes are chemically flexible, and all fibers and surfaces in contact with the precursors are coated with the desired chemistry, creating uniform sites for chemical grafting of amides, silanes, fluorinated groups, and other compounds. These can be finishers or primers for further reaction to improve adhesion, modify electrostatic interaction, surface wetting or appearance, or add biocidal or antimicrobial finishes.

Beyond preparing textiles for further treatment, ALD coatings themselves could make significant improvements in fiber and textile-based products. ALD layers can protect polymers from UV degradation, but cost and throughput will again be key challenges. Biocompatibility is an opportunity area, but more work is needed in ALD/biomaterial interactions. ALD materials could be particularly helpful in advanced filtration technology. A challenge in filtration is to improve capture or chemical degradation capacity while maintaining vapor or liquid throughput for a given pressure drop. 169 Nanoscale ALD coatings on polymer fiber filters can broaden performance in chemically harsh environments while maintaining good species transport performance. Coupling new functional catalysts, including MOF materials, onto fibers will also lead to important new filtration and chemical mitigation applications. Further benefits of ALD coatings for filtration include reduced fouling, reduction or stabilization of static charge, and integration of new materials for real-time chemical sensing capacity.

Further out, future advances in ALD metals, semiconductors, or other materials could enable textile-based switching, energy harvesting, energy storage, information display, chemical or physical sensing, and integrated communication. New low-temperature ALD processes, for example, for piezoelectric, thermoelectric, 170 photoactive perovskite materials, 171 or electropositive metals 172 could be important. MOFs and other porous materials could expand sensing, catalysis, and gas storage applications. Other new chemically active materials could create responsive textiles that change porosity or thermal insulation using input from the environment, or change the look or feel of a material in response to a user's needs.

Along with the need for new materials, more understanding is needed for most every ALD process regarding precursor interactions with natural and synthetic materials, including microscopic processes and mechanisms in vapor infiltration and polymer metalation. Additionally, understanding how ALD textiles function and wear in real-world environments, including machine washing and dry-cleaning, 173 remains a key question and potential challenge. Overcoming these hurdles will help advance many ideas for smart, electronic and multifunctional textiles that promise to change the way we interact with each other and with our environment.
