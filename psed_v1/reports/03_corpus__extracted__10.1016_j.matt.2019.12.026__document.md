## Review

## Atomic Layer Deposition of Two-Dimensional Layered Materials: Processes, Growth Mechanisms, and Characteristics

Jiyu Cai, 1,3 Xiaoxiao Han, 1,2,3 Xin Wang, 1,2,3 and Xiangbo Meng 1,2, *

Since the discovery of graphene, there has been an ever-increasing interest in two-dimensional (2D) layered materials with exceptional properties. To this end, a variety of synthesis methods have been developed. However, it is still challenging to produce large-scale high-quality single-crystalline 2D materials. In this regard, atomic layer deposition (ALD) has recently shown great promise and has stimulated more and more research efforts, ascribed to its unique growth mechanism and distinguished capabilities to achieve nanoscale films with excellent uniformity, unrivaled conformality, and atomic-scale controllability. This review comprehensively summarizes recent progress on ALD for 2D atomic sheets, including 25 different materials and more than 80 ALD processes. This work highlights different technical routes to ALD, their precise controllability, and their underlying principles for 2D materials. It is expected that this work will help boost more research efforts for controllable growth of high-quality 2D materials via ALD.

## INTRODUCTION

Two-dimensional (2D) layered materials are a single layer or multi-layer of atomically stacked structures, featuring van der Waals interaction between adjacent layers but strong covalent bonding within each layer. 1-4 Compared with their bulk counterparts, 2Dmaterials exhibit many exotic physical, chemical, and electronic properties, 5,6 which thereby provide unprecedented opportunities for many emerging applications, such as optical and electronic devices, 7-9 new energy systems, 10,11 catalysis, 12 and bioengineering. 13 In the 20 th century, the scientific community was uncertain about the existence of 2D materials and commonly believed that films become thermodynamically unstable with decreased layers. 14 It was not until 2004 when a monolayer graphene sheet was reported for the first time by Novoselov et al. 15 using a mechanical exfoliation method. This milestone work instantly ignited tremendous research interest, and nowadays, a large variety of 2D materials have been reported, including elements (e.g., graphene, silicene, and germanane), 1,16 nitrides (e.g., h-BN and GaN), 17,18 oxides (e.g., MoO3), 19 chalcogenides (e.g., WS2, MoS2, and SnS x ), 3,6 MXene (e.g., Ti3C2 and Ti4N3), 11 layered organic materials, 20,21 and others.

Beyond the mechanical cleavage of graphene, many new methods have been explored for developing 2D layered materials, such as exfoliation, physical and chemical vapor deposition (PVD and CVD), and solution-based processes, and are well documented in the literature. 5,22-24 However, an ideal method capable of producing large-scale high-quality 2D materials in a controllable manner is still lacking. ## Progress and Potential

Atomic two-dimensional (2D) layered materials are a class of advanced materials with numerous exotic properties. There has been new progress made in 2D materials since singlelayer graphene was exfoliated with Scotch tape in 2004. Over 150 types of 2D materials have been developed to date by various methods. However, it is still challenging to produce largescale high-quality 2D layered structures in a controllable manner. In this regard, recent studies on atomic layer deposition (ALD) have shown many encouraging advances and enabled three technical routes for precisely controllable synthesis of 2D materials at the atomic scale. In this review, we provide a comprehensive survey on ALD efforts and highlight the underlying principles and growth characteristics of ALD processes. The findings from these studies have the potential to provide guidance for designing new ALD processes to produce better 2D materials.

## ALD FUNDAMENTALS AND STRATEGIES FOR 2D MATERIALS

ALD is a well-recognized vapor-phase thin-film technique, credited to Suntola et al. 25 ALD so far has evolved from its initial implication, and its capabilities have been greatly widened for growing a large range of inorganic materials, including elements, oxides, nitrides, sulfides, and ternary and even more complicated compounds. 26,27 As a surface-controlled process, ALD grows films with atomic-scale precision. This is determined by its unique growth mechanism proceeding with self-limiting surface reactions on substrate surfaces. As a result, ALD can produce films with extremely high uniformity, unrivaled conformality, excellent controllability, and at low temperature. 28 To illustrate the ALD growth mechanism, we exemplify the process of ZnS using diethylzinc (DEZ, Zn(C2H5)2) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) as precursors (Figure 1B), 29 in which two half reactions (Equations 1A and 1B) proceed alternatively. Starting from a surface covered by -SH ligands in its initial state, in the ALD process for ZnS, a supply of DEZ results in the first half self-limiting gas-solid surface reaction (Equation 1A) between the -SH ligands and DEZ (step 1). During this step, the ligand-exchange reaction generates a new layer of -Zn(C2H5) species, and releases ethane (C2H6) as the by-product. Once all -SH ligands are depleted, the surface reaction (Equation 1A) completes. In other words, the surface reaction is self-limiting. Then, a purging process (step 2) is applied to clean the overdosed DEZ and the by-product C2H6. Following a thorough purge, a supply of H2S prompts another half self-limiting gas-solid surface reaction (Equation 1B), leading to a new covering of -SH on the surface while releasing C2H6 (step 3). Again, a purging procedure is applied after the second ligand-exchange reaction in order to remove the overdosed H2S and the by-product C2H6 (step 4). Apparently, the two self-limiting half surface reactions are surface controlled by the amount of the surface reactive sites (i.e., -SH and -C2H5). By repeating steps 1-4, the ZnS film grows with increasing ALDcycles. In general, three ALD parameters are adjustable to accomplish desirable materials, i.e., precursor, temperature, and substrate. These fundamentals have been well documented in the literature, and readers may refer to some excellent reviews 26-28,30-32 for more details. In addition to the typical thermal ALD process as illustrated in Figure 1B, there are also some special ALD systems, such as plasmaenhanced ALD (PEALD), 33 particle ALD, 34 spatial ALD, 35 and others.

The second route (R2) (Figure 2B) is a selflimiting layer synthesis (SLS) process, in which the number of layers is determined by the temperature rather than ALD cycles. 36,37 This route has only been used to date for two limited cases: MoS2 36 and WSe2 37 . During the growth of the first layer, precursor molecules chemically adsorb to surface reactive sites on substrates. Once the initial layer is formed over the entire surface, any further chemical adsorption of precursor molecules is hindered due to the absence of suitable adsorption sites on the newly formed 2D atomic layer. Thus, synthesis is forced to proceed with the physical adsorption of precursor molecules. The physical adsorption competes with physical desorption. The net adsorption decreases with increased layers and becomes zero at a critical number of atomic layers, where both the chemical and physical adsorption are disabled. The critical number reduces with increased temperature. This route generally needs high temperatures (e.g., &gt;500 /C14 C, depending on the properties of both precursor and substrate) to enable the self-limiting growth of mono- or few-layer crystalline 2D materials. This strategy has three benefits: (1) the precisely controlled layer number (i.e., mono- to trilayer); (2) highly crystalline

The third route (R3) (Figure 2C) is generally a two-step strategy, in which a precursor film is first developed via ALD at low temperatures and converted into the targeted 2D material via a post-treatment at relatively higher temperatures. For example, metal oxides are first deposited via ALD and then converted into 2D sulfides/selenides via sulfurization/selenization, such as MoS2, 38,39 MoSe2, 40 WS2, 41,42 and Bi2S3. 43 Another example is the production of 2D SnS, in which SnS2 is deposited first via ALD and then reduced in H2 into 2D SnS at high temperature. 44 This R3 route has also been used for h-BN, where a polymer was first deposited via ALD at low temperature and then converted into h-BN in NH3 at high temperature. 45,46 This route has multiple benefits: (1) ease of generalization for more 2D materials; (2) feasible control over the number of layers in the 2D materials; and (3) wafer-sized uniformity. All these growth strategies of ALD offer tremendous opportunities for growing large-scale 2D materials with high quality and high controllability, which are discussed in the following sections in detail.

## ALDPROCESSESANDGROWTHCHARACTERISTICSOF2DMATERIALS

## Graphene

Graphene is an sp 2 -hybridized carbon allotrope with a 2D honeycomb network. 16 So far, it has been fabricated using many approaches, such as exfoliation, CVD, and PVD. 47,48 Zhang et al. 49 deposited graphene on Cu foils via PEALD at 400 /C14 C, using benzene (C6H6) and plasma H2 ( P H2) as precursors, as summarized in Table 1. The six-carbon atomic rings of benzene might have been connected to form graphene via a dehydrogenation process. In the PEALD process, deposition of less than ten ALD cycles resulted in inhomogeneous graphene monolayers, whereas deposition of more than ten cycles led to multilayered graphene sheets. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) has been used to observe the as-deposited films with ten ALD cycles, showing a well-ordered graphene structure (Figure 3A). Raman analyses on the films also revealed a G band at 1,585 cm /C0 1 and a 2D band at 2,687 cm /C0 1 (Figure 3B). The large 2D/G ratio of /C24 1.7 indicated the high quality of the resulting graphene sheets. Moreover, a single-layered graphene within ten ALD cycles has also been determined by selected-area electron diffraction (SAED). However, the single-layered graphene from this work only has a grain size of &lt;10 nm.

## h-BN

Boron nitride (BN) can exist in several polymorphs, such as graphene-like hexagonal (h-BN), diamond-like cubic (c-BN), rhombohedral (r-BN), and wurtzite (w-BN) crystals. 74 h-BN is an analog of graphene, in which B and N atoms are arranged alternately in a 2D plane, and van der Waals forces hold the h-BN layers together. h-BN has many excellent properties, such as wide band gap, high stability, and excellent thermal conductivity. 18 Thus, h-BN films are promising in a wide range of applications 18 and have been synthesized by various methods. 18

The first ALD effort for BN was reported by Marlid et al. 50 in 2002 in a process using BBr3 and NH3 as precursors. They deposited the BN films at 277 /C14 C and then treated them with post-annealing at 727 /C14 C in a vacuum. In addition, Co(0001) (Figure 3D), 61,62 RuO2(110), 63 and Ni(111) 66 substrates were also favorable for achieving crystalline h-BN. In all cases, post-treatment higher than 427 /C14 C in a vacuum was applied. Recently, BCl3 has been used to couple with N2H4 for growing BN via ALD. 68 The anhydrous N2H4 precursor reacted with BCl3 at lower temperature and enabled high BN stoichiometry with low oxygen and carbon impurities. However, the as-grown films were amorphous at 350 /C14 C, which might need a posttreatment for better crystallinity.

Another ALD process of BN used triethylboron (TEB, (C2H5)3B) and NH3 as precursors. In comparison with the processes mentioned above, this new process was not very successful at low temperature ( % 350 /C14 C) 64 but realized self-limiting growth ( /C24 0.7 A ˚ /cycle) using H2 as the carrier gas at 600 /C14 C. 69 TEB has also been used to couple with N2/H2 plasma ( P N2/ P H2) for depositing h-BN. 70 This PEALD study reported appreciable GPCs at temperatures higher than 350 /C14 C. However, the GPCs did not saturate with increased TEB doses, indicating thermal decomposition of TEB. XRD and TEM revealed that the as-deposited BN thin films were polycrystalline with a hexagonal structure in the range of 350 /C14 C450 /C14 C. In addition, post-annealing slightly improved the crystallinity as well as crystallite size.

More recently, several new energy-enhanced processes of BN have been reported with the assistance of plasma, electrons, and hot wires. Park et al. 71 demonstrated a new PEALD process using tris(ethylmethylamino) borane (TEMAB, B(N(CH3)2)3) and plasma NH3 ( P NH3) in the range of 200 /C14 C-350 /C14 C. XRD confirmed the hexagonal structure of the as-deposited films, and HR-TEM revealed that the h-BN stacks were randomly distributed in the as-deposited films without any specific orientation. Creatively, Sprenger et al. 72 developed an electron-enhanced ALD (EEALD) process using borazine (B3N3H6) and electrons as precursors. This EEALD enabled BN to be deposited at room temperature and 100 /C14 C. Electrons stimulated desorption of hydrogen surface species to create reactive dangling bonds, and the dangling bonds facilitated borazine adsorption at low temperatures to form a hBN layer horizontally. After a h-BN monolayer was formed, more borazine could be adsorbed on the h-BN surface for vertical growth. The electron energy range from 80 to 160 eV was favorable to achieve the maximum GPC of /C24 3.2 A ˚ /cycle. The films grown at 100 /C14 C were polycrystalline h-BN and t-BN. However, this EEALD is line-of-sight and therefore not suitable for conformal coatings. Another energy-enhanced ALD was recently reported using a hot wire (tungsten filament), in which B2H6 and NH3 were used as precursors. 73 This hot wire ALD enabled formation of atomic hydrogen and nitrogen-containing radicals by cracking NH3 and deposited B x N y layers in the temperature range of 300 /C14 C-400 /C14 C.

Interestingly but differently, a recent effort reported a two-step ammonia-free ALD for growing BN, in which a pre-ceramic polymer layer was first deposited using trichloroborazine (TCB, H3B3Cl3N3) and hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS, (CH3)3SiNHSi(CH3)3) as precursors and then converted into dense h-BN (Figure 3E). 45 The ALD deposition of BN-based polymer was performed at 80 /C14 C with a GPC of 1.8 A ˚ /cycle, and post-treatment proceeded at 600 /C14 C under Ar for 1 h and then at 1,400 /C14 CunderNH3 for 5 h. The resulting films were confirmed as h-BN with the removal of the organic template. This two-step process has been used to produce structures of h-BN with a high-aspect ratio, such as BN nanotubes (Figures 3F and 3G). 46

## Transition Metal Dichalcogenides

TMDs have a MX2 stoichiometry, and they are stacked layers of transition metal (M) atoms sandwiched between two layers of chalcogen (X is typically S, Se, or Te) atoms. 75 The adjacent layers of TMDs are held together by van der Waals forces. TMDs are commonly found in three polymorphs, i.e., 1T, 2H, and 3R, where the letters stand for trigonal, hexagonal, and rhombohedral, respectively, and the numbers indicate the number of layers in the unit cell (Figure 4A). 76,79 Due to their unique structural characteristics, TMDs have attracted great attention for electronic and optical devices, energy storage, energy conversion, and solid-state lubrication. 2,80

## MoX2

Both MoS2 and MoSe2 are group VIB TMDs, sharing similar crystal structures with three polymorphs, i.e., 1T, 2H, and 3R. 81-84 They are among the most promising TMDs and have many exceptional properties for emerging applications.

MoS2. Recently, MoS2 has been actively investigated in ten ALD processes with different precursors, as summarized in Table 2. Among these ALD processes, Tan et al. 77 reported using MoCl5 and H2S as precursors at 300 /C14 C. The proposed surface chemistry is illustrated in Figure 4B. The as-grown MoS2 films were crystalline (inset in Figure 4B), and a post-treatment in sulfur vapor improved their crystallinity. It was found that annealing at 800 /C14 C helped reconstruct the MoS2 films with thinner thickness and higher crystallinity. For example, annealing changed the film thickness from 1.7 nm with ten-cycle ALD MoS2 to 0.8 nm and from 3.2 nm with 20-cycle ALD MoS2 to 1.3 nm. The annealed films of ten-cycle ALD MoS2 show 2m m triangular crystals (Figure 4C), whereas thicker ALD-deposited films helped produce continuous films after annealing. Using the same precursors, several subsequent studies also confirmed the growth of MoS2 films in the range of 350 /C14 C-475 /C14 C. 85-88 Interestingly but differently, Kim et al. 36 even applied much higher growth temperatures in the range of 500 /C14 C-900 /C14 C. After 120 ALD cycles at growth temperatures of 500 /C14 C, 700 /C14 C, and 900 /C14 C, the as-deposited films were 2, 1.4, and 0.8 nm thick (corresponding to the thickness of tri-, bi-, and monolayer MoS2), 98 respectively. Another well-investigated precursor pair for ALD MoS2 is Mo(CO)6/H2S. This ALD process features the high vapor of Mo(CO)6 at room temperature and relatively low growth temperature. ## TiS 2

TiS2 is a group IVB TMD material with two crystal structures: 1T and 2H (Figure 8A). 1T is the most stable polytype, and 2H is the metastable phase. 115 TiS2 has many intriguing properties, 115,120 which make it a promising material for a number of applications. 121-123 To date, only one ALD process has been reported for growing 2D TiS2 films, as summarized in Table 5. In this regard, Pore et al. 116 investigated an ALD for TiS2 at 400 /C14 C, using TiCl4 and H2S as precursors. In the study, they noticed that substrates affected the growth of TiS2. The TiS2 films were nearly all plate-like crystallites parallel to the ZnS substrate (Figure 8B), whereas the films were randomly oriented on soda-lime glass (Figure 8C). XRD revealed that the TiS2 films on ZnS were highly (001) oriented, whereas the ones on soda-lime glass showed multiple peaks. ZrS2 is also a group IVB TMD material with 1T and 2H phases. 125 The 1T ZrS2 (Figure 8G) is semiconducting and has many exotic properties for various applications. 118,119,126-129 The only study on ALD for ZrS2 was reported in the latest work by Mattinen et al., 119 in which both ZrS2 and HfS2 thin films were investigated due to their analogous crystal structures and lattice parameters. ## HfS2

HfS2 is another group IVB TMD analogous to ZrS2. The only ALD work on HfS2 was reported together with the ALD for ZrS2 discussed above. 119 In the work, three precursor pairs, HfCl4/H2S, HfI4/H2S, and Hf(NEtMe)4/H2S, were examined; the HfCl4/H2S pair has been mainly investigated. 119 Using HfCl4 and H2S as precursors (Figure 8H), the HfS2 films were deposited in the range of 200 /C14 C 500 /C14 C. It was revealed that the HfS2 films were amorphous at low deposition temperatures ( % 350 /C14 C) with chlorine residues, while crystalline 1T HfS2 films were obtained at high deposition temperatures between 350 /C14 Cand500 /C14 C. However, XRD peaks of monoclinic HfO2 were also seen for the HfS2 films deposited at 450 /C14 C or higher. In comparison, the HfS2 films deposited at 400 /C14 C exhibited better crystallinity and purity.

In the work, HfI4 was also tried in place of HfCl4 for ALD of HfS2. 119 This ALD process enabled 1T HfS2 at 300 /C14 C, but the HfS2 surface from HfI4/H2S was rougher. Alternatively, Hf(NEtMe)4 was studied, but the as-deposited HfS2 films had poor crystallinity, non-uniform coverage, and inhomogeneous composition.

## ReS2

ReS2 is a 2D TMD with distorted octahedral (1T 0 ) monolayer structure. 130 The distorted 1T 0 phase structure induces inherent reduced crystal symmetry, leading to strongly in-plane anisotropic optical and transport properties. ReS2 has also exhibited extraordinary layer-independent properties, which make it attractive in many applications. 130,131 Only one ALD for ReS2 has been reported to date using ReCl5 and H2S as precursors (Table 5). 124 In the work, Ha ¨ ma ¨ la ¨ inen et al. revealed that the growth of ReS2 had a strong dependency on temperature. In the range of 120 /C14 C-200 /C14 C, the GPC of ReS2 increased from /C24 0.3 A ˚ /cycle to /C24 0.9 A ˚ /cycle, the stoichiometry of the films improved from a S/Re ratio of 1-2, and the crystallinity evolved from amorphous to crystalline. At higher temperatures, the GPC was nearly unchanged at 0.8-0.9 A ˚ /cycle at 200 /C14 C-300 /C14 C but then gradually decreased to /C24 0.2 A ˚ /cycle at 450 /C14 C. The ReS2 films remained crystalline and stoichiometric above 200 /C14 C, while the crystalline orientation varied with temperature. It was observed that between 200 /C14 C and 300 /C14 C, the ReS2 crystallites were parallel to the substrate, while above 350 /C14 C, the plate-like crystallites had more random orientations toward the substrate. Overall, high-quality crystalline and stoichiometric ReS2 films can be obtained above 200 /C14 C, and the crystal orientation is tunable by adjusting the deposition temperature.

## GeX

Germanium monochalcogenides (GeX: GeS, GeSe, and GeTe) are group IVA compounds. To overcome SnS2 impurities, Jang et al. 44 reported a simple and novel R3 approach to obtain single-phase SnS thin films. This approach enabled high phase purity, good surface smoothness, and good adhesion to SiO2 substrates.

SnS2. Thimsen et al. 169 made the first attempt on ALD of SnS2, which involved a trilayer stack of binary metal sulfides (Cu2S, SnS2, and ZnS). ## Sb2Te3

Sb2Te3 is a group VA chalcogenide with cubic and rhombohedral phases. 174,175 In layered rhombohedral Sb2Te3, the unit cell consists of three quintuple layers (QL), and each individual QL has atoms covalently bonded in the following atomic arrangement: Te-Sb-Te-Sb-Te (Figure 11A). 175,178 Sb2Te3 exhibits excellent p -type thermoelectric properties at room temperature and is widely used as a topological insulator. On the other hand, Sb2Te3 has a crystalline-amorphous phase transition at 140 /C14 C, showing promise for phase-change memory devices. 179 Consequently, many efforts have been invested in developing 2D Sb2Te3, and multiple ALD processes have been developed to date (Table 9).

Pore et al. 142 investigated an ALD process of Sb2Te3 using SbCl3 and a tellurium alkylsilyl compound ((Et3Si)2Te). The GPC was /C24 0.6 A ˚ /cycle at 60 /C14 C but dropped to 0.17 A ˚ /cycle at 90 /C14 C and 0.02 A ˚ /cycle at 140 /C14 C. The resultant Sb2Te3 films were the rhombohedral phase. Similarly, Hatanpa ¨ a ¨ et al. 141 and Zastrow et al. 180 reported a GPC of 0.57 A ˚ /cycle at 60 /C14 C and 80 /C14 C, respectively. Knapas et al. 145 confirmed repeatable growth between the two precursors using in situ QCM.

In another ALD process, SbCl3 was coupled with (trimethylsilyl) telluride ((Me3Si)2Te) as precursors. 181 Gu et al. 181 found that the optimal deposition temperatures were between 60 /C14 Cand70 /C14 Cin order to produce relatively smooth polycrystalline Sb2Te3 thin films. Higher temperatures were prone to cause precursor desorption and resulted in discontinuous Sb2Te3 films. In addition, substrates had evident effects on the growth of Sb2Te3. ## Bi 2X3

Bi 2 S3 . In group VA, there are three bismuth chalcogenides reported by ALD, i.e., Bi2S3, Bi2Se3, and Bi2Te3. Bi2S3 has the layered orthorhombic and rhombohedral structures. 190 The orthorhombic phase is thermodynamically stable under ambient conditions. 191 Liu et al. 43 first reported an ALD process for growing Bi2S3 films via an R3 route. The crystalline Bi2O3 films were first deposited via an ALD using Bi(thd)3 (thd = 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-3,5-heptanedionato) and H2O precursors at 300 /C14 C and were then converted to orthorhombic Bi2S3 through a post-sulfurization process in sulfur vapor at 500 /C14 C. Subsequently, Mahuli et al. 185 used Bi(thd)3 and H2S to grow Bi2S3 films directly via ALD in the range of 125 /C14 C-300 /C14 C. The GPC was constant

(0.34-0.37 A ˚ /cycle) in the range of 175 /C14 C-250 /C14 C. Otherwise, the GPC was lower at lower and higher temperatures, caused by either limited reactivity or increased desorption of precursors. The as-deposited Bi2S3 films at 200 /C14 C were the polycrystalline orthorhombic phase with high purity.

Bi 2 Se3. Bi2Se3 has two polytypes: 2D rhombohedral and 1D orthorhombic phases. 192,193 The layered rhombohedral phase is formed by QLs, i.e., five hexagonal close-packed atomic sublayers (Se-Bi-Se-Bi-Se). 193 Bi2Se3 is a unique semiconductor with a narrow band gap of /C24 0.3 eV and thus promising in many applications, such as optical recording systems, photoelectrochemical devices, and thermoelectric devices. 194 The early ALD studies were conducted on Bi2Se3 by Pore et al. 142 and Hatanpa ¨ a ¨ et al., 141 using BiCl3 and (Et3Si)2Se at 165 /C14 C. They revealed that a GPC of 0.97 A ˚ /cycle could be achieved, and the films had a good stoichiometry of 41% Bi and 59% Se. In 2015, Sarnet et al. 186 conducted a thorough study on this process in the range of 160 /C14 C-350 /C14 C. They found that the film growth had a strong dependence on temperature, dropping from 1.6 A ˚ /cycle at 160 /C14 C to 0.1 A ˚ /cycle at 250 /C14 C. At 275 /C14 C and 300 /C14 C, the as-deposited films were discontinuous. In the entire temperature range, the films had high stoichiometry. The films at 160 /C14 C were polycrystalline with a major rhombohedral phase and minor metastable orthorhombic (1D) phase.

Bi 2 Te3. Bi2Te3 has a layered rhombohedral structure formed by QLs where atoms follow the sequence Te-Bi-Te-Bi-Te. 195 It has high electrical conductivity, low thermal conductivity, and a band gap of 0.16 eV. 196 Pore et al. 142 and Hatanpa ¨a ¨ et al. 141 made the first attempt on ALD Bi2Te3 using BiCl3 and (Et3Si)2Te at 165 /C14 C. Then, Sarnet et al. 187 carried out a systematic investigation on the process in the range of 160 /C14 C350 /C14 C. It was interesting that the Bi2Te3 ALD showed much similarity in growth characteristics as the Bi2Se3 ALD using BiCl3 and (Et3Si)2Te. 186 This might be attributed to the similar properties of the precursors. The as-deposited films were the polycrystalline rhombohedral phase with high stoichiometry. Another precursor set for ALD Bi2Te3 is BiCl3 and (Me3Si)2Te, reported by Zhang et al. 176 and Nminibapiel et al. 182 at 165 /C14 C and 170 /C14 C, respectively. They commonly disclosed that the initial nucleation of the Bi2Te3 thin film was an island growth mode, leading to the vertical growth of islands and clusters rather than lateral growth. Both annealing and longer ALD precursor dose times improved film coverage. In addition, the two studies also reported that the as-deposited Bi2Te3 films were the layered rhombohedral structure. In a subsequent work, Bae et al. 188 further clarified the Bi2Te3 growth characteristics and film properties in the range of 100 /C14 C-160 /C14 C. They disclosed that the GPC increased with temperature from 0.4 A ˚ /cycle at 100 /C14 C to 1.3 A ˚ /cycle at 160 /C14 C. The as-deposited Bi2Te3 films were the polycrystalline rhombohedral phase.

In order to overcome the high source temperature (140 /C14 C) of BiCl3 and avoid the formation of corrosive HCl, Rusek et al. 189 explored five homoleptic Bi precursors to pair with (Et3Si)2Te for growing Bi2Te3 via ALD. Based on the results from differential scanning calorimetry and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, (Me2N)3Bi, (MeEtN)3Bi, and [(Me3Si)2NBim -NSiMe3]2 showed high thermal stability and high reactivity with (Et3Si)2Te at relatively low temperatures. All the resultant Bi2Te3 films using the three Bi precursors yielded high stoichiometry and the polycrystalline rhombohedral structure with perpendicularly oriented crystallites to the substrates. In addition, Bi2Te3 films from [(Me3Si)2NBim -NSiMe3]2 at 170 /C14 C exhibited better flatness and smoothness on Si and SiO2 substrates, and the GPCs were 1.1 and 1.2 A ˚ /cycle on Si and SiO2 substrates, respectively. ## WO3

WO3 can exhibit five phases related to temperature, i.e., low temperature monoclinic ( ε -WO3, /C0 140 /C14 C to /C0 50 /C14 C), triclinic ( d -WO3, /C0 50 /C14 C to 17 /C14 C), room temperature monoclinic ( g -WO3, 17 /C14 C-330 /C14 C), orthorhombic ( b -WO3, 330 /C14 C-740 /C14 C), and tetragonal structure ( a -WO3, &gt;740 /C14 C). 218-220 g -WO3 is the most stable phase at room temperature and has a layered structure. Recent ALD efforts for WO3 are summarized in Table 10. Liu et al. 221 first demonstrated an ALD process using (N t Bu)2(NMe2)2W and H2O as precursors. The deposition was performed on indium tin oxide-coated glass substrates at 250 /C14 C-350 /C14 C with a GPC of 1 A ˚ /cycle. The as-deposited WO3 films were oxygen deficient and a post-annealing process at 550 /C14 C in O2 helped achieve stoichiometric WO3 with improved crystallinity. HR-TEM analyses confirmed that the annealed WO3 films had a well-crystallized monoclinic structure. This process has been used to grow WO3 thin films for photodetectors. 213-215

W(CO)6 is another widely used W precursor, with ample vapor and high reactivity. Coupling W(CO)6 with O3, Malm et al. 208 realized the deposition of WO3 via ALD in the range of 160 /C14 C-225 /C14 C, with a GPC varying from /C24 0.05 to 0.28 A ˚ /cycle. ## CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK

Since the delivery of graphene in 2004, there has been an ever-increasing interest in 2D layered structures, and to date, over 150 different 2D materials have been reported. 222 These atomically thin 2D nanosheets have exhibited numerous exotic properties and therefore are promising in a large variety of emerging applications. In pursing these 2D materials, various methods have been developed (e.g., exfoliation, CVD, PVD, and wet chemistry) 22 , determinant to their quality, cost, and applications. So far, however, it is still challenging for the many different methods to produce large-scale high-quality 2D atomic layers in a controllable manner. In this context, ALD has recently been investigated as a new technique for synthesizing 2D materials, especially in the past 5 years, featuring accurate controllability over material composition and crystallinity, atomic-scale precision of material

<!-- image -->

growth, film uniformity and conformality over any shaped substrates, and at low temperatures even down to room temperature. The recent ALD efforts have exhibited these exceptional capabilities in producing 2D materials.

In this review, we have carried out a comprehensive survey on 2D layered materials synthesized by ALD, including 25 types of 2D materials and over 80 ALD processes. We have categorized the technical strategies of ALD studies into three routes. R1 is the typical ALD process, in which 2D materials are deposited into an amorphous phase, a polycrystalline phase, or a mixture of the two. The film crystallinity is related to the properties of the precursors and the substrates and the temperature. Annealing is a general measure to further improve the crystallinity of the resulting films. Most of the ALD processes reported to date are in this category. R2 is a special ALD phenomenon and has not been well studied. To date, only two studies have been reported for this technical route, i.e., MoS2 using MoCl5/H2S in the range of 500 /C14 C-900 /C14 C 36 and WSe2 using WCl6/DESe in the range of 600 /C14 C-800 /C14 C. 37 The two studies were from the same group. This technical route features self-limiting growth of several layers of 2D materials. The number of layers of 2D materials is dependent on the deposition temperature but not the number of ALD cycles. It is different from R1; R2 enables higher quality of 2D layers, and its success is related to the formation of high-quality 2D layers lacking dangling bonds on the layer surface. This technical route has realized excellent layer uniformity up to 95% over large areas at wafer-level scale. Thus, R2 is promising, but its exact mechanism remains unclear. In the two successful cases of MoS2 and WSe2 via R2, the two ALD processes both used metal halides (MoCl5 and WCl6) as the metal sources and high deposition temperatures above 500 /C14 C. The metal halides have high reactivity and high thermal stability. The two coupled precursors (H2S and DESe, respectively) also have the same characteristics with regard to reactivity and stability. Thus, we postulate that R2 requires precursors with high reactivity and thermal stability and needs to proceed at high deposition temperatures. Based on the two studies, other 2D materials via R2 may be possible by adopting similar precursors (e.g., WCl6/H2S for WS2) and performing at high temperatures (e.g., above 500 /C14 C). In this regard, more studies are apparently needed. Of the three technical routes, R3 has some distinctive advantages and has been used for six 2D materials (h-BN, 45,46 MoS2, 38,39 MoSe2, 40 WS2, 41,42 SnS, 44 and Bi2S3 43 ) with eight ALD processes. In this technical route, ALD processes are used to deposit precursor films, which later convert into targeted 2D materials via a post-treatment (e.g., sulfurization, 38,39 selenization, 40 or reduction 44 ) at high temperatures. In comparison, R3 is versatile and easy to perform for high-quality 2D films with controlled layer numbers over wafer-scale substrates. These advantages distinguish R3 from R1 and R2. Based on these benefits, R3 has great potential for large-scale high-quality 2D films with controllable layers.

Besides categorizing the different ALD processes into three technical routes, another contribution of this review lies in the systematic summary of all the ALD processes. The data in the ten tables will allow readers to examine the important parameters of the ALD processes for 2D materials. They also are a valuable source for researchers to track ALD studies in this area with ease. The third contribution of this work lies in the summary of the underlying mechanisms and growth characteristics of the different ALD processes. These are expected to help prompt new efforts for developing new precursors and for widening the applications of ALD. In summary, the review offers an express way for researchers to familiarize themselves with the ALD methodologies and processes for 2D materials. For future studies, we expect more excitement and successes in the following aspects.

## New ALD Precursors and Processes

Precursors have a vital role in ALD processes. Their properties determine the success of an ALD process, such as reactivity, thermal stability, and volatility. In this regard, new precursors are urgently needed to expand ALD's capabilities for 2D materials. Ideally, they should enable high reactivity, high thermal stability, and high volatility. On the other hand, new precursors that can be produced with ease and cost-effectively are preferred. In this regard, we expect that there will be new precursors enabling low-temperature deposition of large-scale uniform well-crystallized 2D materials.

## In Situ and Advanced Characterizations

Many phenomena have not been well understood during ALD processes. To this end, in situ lab-based techniques are valuable tools in ALD studies, such as FTIR, QCM, QMS, ellipsometry, and XPS. 223-225 Combinations of these techniques provide insightful data on materials growth during ALD processes, which will be essential for us to understand the related surface chemistry, chemical composition, and structure of materials. For example, these lab-based techniques can explain the evolution of growth rates with temperature and the changes in surface reactions. In addition to these lab-based techniques, in situ synchrotron-based X-ray techniques provide us with a suite of advanced characterizations, 226 including XRD, XRR, XPS, X-ray fluorescence, and X-ray absorption. They are particularly powerful to secure film thickness, roughness, crystallinity, composition, and chemical state, featuring their shorter acquisition time and higher precision than those in the labbased techniques. They are sensitive to changes on the atomic scale and nanoscale. In this regard, these techniques should be able to provide solutions to explain the evolution of film crystallinity, chemical state, and chemical composition with film thickness, temperature, and substrate. Furthermore, in situ TEM is another important tool for characterizing the evolution of materials microstructures with temperature during ALD processes. 227 All these in situ tools are particularly significant for ALD studies, and they can greatly help advance our understanding on advanced materials and the fundamentals of the related chemistry.

## New Applications

With the expected progress in ALD processes and materials characterization, it can be foreseen that we will be able to produce large-scale high-quality 2D materials in a precise and controllable manner. Therefore, we would further expect that there will be a dramatic increase in applications with ALD-deposited 2D materials, such as in catalysis, energy, biomedicine, sensors, electronics, and semiconductors, ascribed to the excellent capabilities of ALD in producing 2D materials.

With this review, we expect that readers will benefit greatly in the search for highquality 2D materials with a feasible ALD process. We also expect that there will be more interest sparked among researchers to use ALD as an important tool for developing new 2D materials for emerging applications.
