A reactor tube and a nanocrystal film solve the same equation

Two ALD transport models, twelve years and six orders of magnitude of length scale apart. Written side by side they turn out to be the same advection–diffusion–reaction system — and the “excess number” of the 2012 reactor paper is exactly the reciprocal of the capacity ratio that governs infill. Here is the algebra, and the numerical check.

Three substitutions collapse one into the other

Make those three swaps and the surface equations become character-for-character identical, and the gas sinks become the same expression. What is left over is a single term: the reactor has advection u ∂n/∂z, the film does not.

The check

Same groups in, same profile out

Both solvers were run at Pe = 0, Da = 955, γ = 9.85 × 10−6 to τ = 1.78 × 104 (a 60.7 ms dose on the 1 µm film) — the infill solver on its physical finite-volume grid, and a direct implementation of Eqs. (9)–(10) on a dimensionless one.

Only the advection term changes the mathematics. The rest are the same equation evaluated at different coefficients — and the last row is the one the reactor model has no analogue for, because a reactor tube does not fill itself in.

Verdict

Same equation, different regime

Do they boil down to the same result?

The equations: yes. Up to the coverage convention and the advection term, they are one system. Fed the same (Pe, Da, γ) the two independent solvers return the same profile to 4.9e-04 in coverage — and the porosity term that appears in one and not the other changes the answer by less than 4e-06, because at γ << 1 gas-phase accumulation is negligible either way.

The regimes: far apart, and not where you would guess. Da is comparable (955 vs 1550), so both show a sharp front rather than a gradient. γ differs by a factor of 2.5 × 105 — a reactor tube holds a couple of precursor molecules per surface site, a nanocrystal film about one per hundred thousand. Yet the dose times come out within an order of magnitude of each other: 173 ms to drive the front through the film against 1.75 s through the reactor. Both scale as L2/(2γD), and the film being 4.5 × 105 shorter very nearly repays its 2.5 × 105 deficit in γ.

What does not cancel is the last row of the table above. A reactor tube has the same radius on cycle 500 as on cycle 1; a nanocrystal film is busy sealing its own pores, so Sv, rp and De all collapse as it densifies and the dose requirement climbs by orders of magnitude across a run. That is the one question the reactor model structurally cannot be asked.

Practically: the reactor paper’s three groups are the right coordinates for the infill problem too, and the reconstruction of paper B lands inside the family paper A defined. That is reassuring, but it is not the same as knowing what paper B wrote — a readable copy would still settle the closures it chose for Sv(ε) and pore closure.
