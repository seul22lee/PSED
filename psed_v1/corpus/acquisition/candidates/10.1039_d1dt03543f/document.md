## Introduction

In the past decades, atomic layer deposition (ALD) has become an indispensable tool for nanotechnology. 1,2 It relies on selflimiting chemical reactions between a sample surface and gasphase precursors, which enables the synthesis of inorganic materials with atomic precision. ALD is traditionally achieved by alternating metal precursor ( e.g. a metalorganic compound) and co-reactant ( e.g. H2O or O2 gas/plasma) in a two-step process. In ALD of elemental thin films, in a simplified view the metal precursor first binds to the surface through saturated chemisorption, after which the co-reactant removes all of the ligands from the surface except for the intended element itself. So far, ALD of elemental films has been demonstrated almost exclusively for noble metals. ## Atomic layer deposition of ternary ruthenates by combining metalorganic precursors with RuO4 as the co-reactant †

<!-- image -->

Matthias M. Minjauw, a Ji-Yu Feng, a Timo Sajavaara, b Christophe Detavernier a and Jolien Dendooven * a

<!-- image -->

In this work, the use of ruthenium tetroxide (RuO4) as a co-reactant for atomic layer deposition (ALD) is reported. The role of RuO4 as a co-reactant is twofold: it acts both as an oxidizing agent and as a Ru source. It is demonstrated that ALD of a ternary Ru-containing metal oxide ( i.e. a metal ruthenate) can be achieved by combining a metalorganic precursor with RuO4 in a two-step process. RuO4 is proposed to combust the organic ligands of the adsorbed precursor molecules while also binding RuO2 to the surface. As a proof of concept two metal ruthenate processes are developed: one for aluminum ruthenate, by combining trimethylaluminum (TMA) with RuO4; and one for platinum ruthenate, by combining MeCpPtMe3 with RuO4. Both processes exhibit self-limiting surface reactions and linear growth as a function of the number of ALD cycles. The observed saturated growth rates are relatively high compared to what is usually the case for ALD. At 100 °C sample temperature, growth rates of 0.86 nm per cycle and 0.52 nm per cycle are observed for the aluminum and platinum ruthenate processes, respectively. The TMA/RuO4 process results in a 1 : 1 Al to Ru ratio, while the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process yields a highly Rurich composition with respect to Pt. It oxidizes the ligands of incoming precursor molecules during the next precursor pulse, liberating electrons to reduce the metal center. 5 Similar combustion chemistries have been reported for ALD of Ru combining a metalorganic precursor with O2. 6 Alternatively, Ru ALD can be achieved using the inorganic RuO4 precursor in combination with H2 gas or plasma as coreactant at 100 °C. 7,8 The RuO4 precursor oxidizes the Ru surface of the growing film, while at the same time depositing RuO2 in a saturated manner. During the H2 pulse the surface is reduced back to metallic Ru.

In ALD of binary compounds, such as metal oxides or metal nitrides, next to the removal of the ligands of the supplies the second element ( e.g. the oxygen or nitrogen atoms). A prototypical process is thermal ALD of Al2O3, where the Al species are supplied by Al(CH)3 (trimethylaluminum or TMA) and the O species by H2O. The surface chemistry in this case uses self-limiting ligand exchange reactions in an acid -base chemistry. 5,9

Next to elemental and binary materials, the deposition of ternary and quaternary materials by ALD has attracted a lot of attention. 10 The most common way to deposit ternary or quaternary materials is to combine multiple binary ALD processes into supercycles. Considering growth of ternary ruthenium oxides, Cheon et al. demonstrated ALD of RuAlO at 230 °C by combining ALD of Al2O3 and ALD of Ru in an alternating fashion, yielding a nanocomposite. 11 The Al2O3 ALD subcycles consisted of pulses of TMA and H2O, while the Ru ALD subcycles consisted of pulses of IMBCHRu [( η 6-1-isopropyl-4methylbenzene)( η 4-cyclohexa-1,3-diene)Ru(0), C15H22Ru] and O2 gas. The microstructure of the resulting films comprised Ru nanocrystals separated by amorphous Al2O3, displaying enhanced Cu di /uniFB00 usion barrier properties compared to the columnar microstructure typically achieved for pure Ru ALD films. Han et al. demonstrated SrRuO3 growth at 230 °C by combining ALD of SrO, using the Sr(iPr3Cp)2 precursor with O2 gas, with the thermal decomposition of RuO4 to RuO2 on the SrO surface. 12 Note, however, that the latter is not a selfsaturating reaction. Next to ternary ruthenium oxides, ternary ruthenium nitrides have been deposited by ALD, also by using the supercycle approach. 13,14

The supercycle approach in principle o /uniFB00 ers excellent composition control by tuning the fraction of each binary process within the supercycle, but several challenges are encountered. 10 As ALD processes for di /uniFB00 erent materials are alternated, overlapping ALD temperature windows are required to achieve saturated conditions and nucleation e /uniFB00 ects of one material on the other might occur, which can be di /uniFB03 cult to control. In the specific case of Ru-containing oxides, the use of strong oxidants such as O2 plasma in the sequence can pose challenges because it can lead to etching of deposited Ru species as volatile RuO4, 15 impacting composition and morphology control. Finally, if two successive binary materials do not mix well, one might end up with a multilayer or mixture of two separate material phases.

Alternative approaches to deposit ternary materials by ALD include co-dosing of precursors and use of a multiconstituent precursor. Such a precursor has multiple metal centres and therefore several elements are introduced on the surface during the same precursor pulse. 16 The co-reactant can also be multiconstituent, which is for example the case when it acts as oxidizing agent and metal source, as demonstrated before for metal alkoxides. 17 Rimoldi et al. reported the growth of rhenium aluminium oxide thin films by alternating TMA and ReO3Me. 18 This process was later investigated in detail by Gebhard et al. 19 They reported the growth of ReAl2O3CH3 with an exceptionally high growth rate of 0.45 nm per cycle at 150 °C. The mechanistic studies revealed that catalytic chemistry at the Re center occurs during ALD, yielding hydrogen and olefins as reaction products. To the best of our knowledge, routes based on multiconstituent reactants have not been reported for ALD of ternary ruthenium oxides.

In this work, we will demonstrate that RuO4 can be used as a multiconstituent co-reactant in a two-step ALD process for the growth of metal ruthenates at substrate temperatures below 125 °C. In this ALD-route, RuO4 acts as both the oxidizing agent and the Ru-source. As a proof of concept, we developed two-step ALD processes for aluminum and platinum ruthenate, by combining trimethylaluminum (TMA) or (methylcyclopenta-dienyl)trimethylplatinum (MeCpPtMe3) with RuO4, respectively. As metal ruthenates present an intriguing class of fundamentally and technologically relevant ternary oxides, 20 -22 due to their interesting electrical and magnetic properties, and excellent thermal and chemical stability, the presented method is expected to create opportunities for fields in search of conformally deposited ultrathin ruthenate thin films, for example, as solid-state (electro)catalyst or electrode materials.

## Experimental

## TMA/RuO4 process

The ALD-reactor used for the aluminum ruthenate depositions is a home-built vacuum system, equipped with a turbomolecular pump and an oil-based backing pump, allowing the system to achieve a base pressure of 10 -7 mbar. The used precursors for the aluminum ruthenate depositions were trimethylaluminum (TMA, Strem Chemicals, min. 98%) and RuO4 (ToRuS ™ : solution of RuO4 in an inert organic solvent, Air Liquide). Both TMA and RuO4 were stored in stainless steel vessels at room temperature, and were pulsed into the chamber by vapor draw through stainless steel tubing and pneumatically controlled inlets. The tubing was heated to 35 °C for both TMA and RuO4. The pressure in the ALD chamber during the TMA and RuO4 pulsing was set to 0.005 mbar and 0.02 mbar, respectively, by using needle valves at the precursor inlets.

## MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process

The ALD-reactor used for the platinum ruthenate depositions is a home-built vacuum system, allowing for synchrotronbased in situ XRF and GISAXS measurements. 23 The vacuum chamber is equipped with a turbomolecular pump backed by an oil-based rotation pump, allowing the system to reach a base pressure of 10 -6 mbar. The two-step process is performed by alternating exposures of (methylcyclo-pentadienyl)trimethylplatinum (MeCpPtMe3) (Strem Chemicals, 99%) and RuO4 (ToRuS ™ : solution of RuO4 in an inert organic solvent, Air Liquide). Both precursors are vapor drawn into the ALD reactor through heated stainless steel tubing. For the Pt precursor Ar carrier gas was used, while no carrier gas was used for ToRuS. The Pt precursor is kept in a glass vessel at 35 °C, with the tubing at 45 °C. The RuO4 precursor is kept in a stainless steel vessel at room temperature (no heating), with the tubing at 50 °C. The precursor pulsing in this process was of a static nature. This means that during each pulse, the gate valve between the ALD chamber and the turbo pump was closed, the precursors were injected for 6 s until a high pressure was achieved (Pt: 1 mbar, Ru: 2 mbar). After 9 s at this ' static ' pressure (the total pulse time is therefore 15 s), the chamber

was pumped down to base pressure again by first bypassing the chamber to the rotary pump, before opening the gate valve towards the turbo pump. In the three-step ALD process, static H2 (20% H2 in Ar) with a pressure of 4 mbar is added following these two precursor pulses. In this case, the injection time for H2 was 8 s and the time at 4 mbar static pressure 20 s, giving a total pulse time of 28 s.

## In situ characterization during the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process

In situ XRF measurements were performed at the DUBBLE BM26B beamline of the ESRF synchrotron. The samples were irradiated by a 11.98 kV beam at an incidence angle of 0.5° in between ALD cycles, and the fluorescent X-rays were detected by a Vortex silicon drift detector. From the XRF data, both in situ and ex situ (see below), an equivalent thickness of Pt and Ru was extracted. This was done by first constructing calibration curves of the Pt M and Ru L peak intensities versus the XRR thickness of sputtered Pt and Ru films, respectively, and afterwards using these calibration curves to extract an equivalent Pt or Ru thickness of the ALD films based on the XRF data.

## Thin film characterization

X-ray reflectometry (XRR) and X-ray di /uniFB00 raction (XRD) measurements were performed on a Bruker D8 di /uniFB00 ractometer using Cu K α radiation. The film thickness and density were determined by fitting simulated XRR patterns to the acquired reflectometry data. Ex situ X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF) was performed using a Bruker ARTAX μ -XRF spectrometer. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed on an FEI Quanta 200F instrument. EDX spectra were acquired by scanning a 5 kV electron beam onto a large surface area, and using an EDAX silicondrift detector. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Thermo Scientific ™ Theta Probe instrument using monochromated Al K α X-rays generated at 15 kV, and focused into a 300 μ m spot by the Si crystal monochromator mirror. The concentric hemispherical analyzer was set to a pass energy of 200 eV. Depth profiling was performed using an EX05 Ar + ion gun. Time-of-flight elastic recoil detection analysis (TOF-ERDA) was performed using 8 MeV 35 Cl 4+ incident ions from the 1.7 MV Pelletron accelerator. The TOF-ERDA data were analysed by means of Potku software.

## MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process

To illustrate the generality of the presented approach, platinum ruthenates were deposited as well, combining MeCpPtMe3 and RuO4. The growth was carried out in a dedicated ALD setup enabling synchrotron-based in situ characterization of the process at the DUBBLE BM26 beamline of the ESRF. Fig. 4a displays the growth curve obtained from in situ X-ray fluorescence (XRF) measurements, and reveals that linear growth is achieved after a few cycles of non-linear growth at the start of the process. It is clear that the number of Ru atoms (right y -axis) deposited in each cycle is larger than the number of Pt atoms (left y -axis). Importantly, the Pt to Ru ratio seems to be relatively stable during film growth. The ex situ XRR data acquired on this 50 cycle sample are shown in Fig. 4b. The pattern is consistent with a uniform, low-roughness film of 25.9 nm deposited onto Si with a density of approximately 6 g cm -3 . This value lies closer to the bulk density of RuO2, 6.97 g cm -3 , than that of PtO2, 10.5 g cm -3 , in line with a Ru-rich composition. Based on the geometrical layer thickness as determined by XRR, we can estimate the growth rate of the process at 0.52 nm per cycle.

The saturation behaviour of the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process was investigated, confirming saturated growth for a 15 s MeCpPtMe3 exposure time (Fig. 5a). The growth rate of the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process as a function of RuO4 exposure is shown in Fig. 5b. In this case, the increase in GPC clearly levels o /uniFB00 at 45 s RuO4 exposure. As this corresponds to three successive static RuO4 pulses of 15 s, and this is unfavorable towards the time e /uniFB03 ciency of the process and the precursor consumption, a single 15 s RuO4 pulse was selected for further experiments. In this way, it was found that the growth rate of the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process increases with substrate temperature, from 0.26 nm per cycle at 40 °C to 0.54 nm per cycle at 120 °C (Fig. 5c).

Pt, Ru and O concentrations of respectively 2.51 ± 0.06 at%, 16.2 ± 0.5 at% and 64 ± 2 at% were extracted, while ERD also provides the concentrations of the light element impurities. The carbon and hydrogen content was slightly lower than for the TMA/RuO4 deposited thin film, i.e. 7.5 ± 0.9 at% and 3.5 ± 0.4 at%, respectively. While for the TMA/ RuO4 process trace amounts below 1 at% were found in the film, about 6.0 ± 0.7 at% of fluorine was incorporated in the film grown with the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process.

Overall, the results obtained for the MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process show promise that the RuO4-based ALD-route presented here can be applicable to a wide variety of metal precursors, yielding MRuO x or M-doped RuO x thin films. The Ru-rich nature of the deposited thin films may be related to the specific ligands of the Pt precursor molecule. If several RuO4 molecules react with the remaining ligands of a single adsorbed Pt precursor molecule at the surface, this can explain the high proportion of Ru relative to Pt in the resulting films as well as the high growth rate observed for the process, i.e. 0.52 nm per cycle at 120 °C. More insights in the fundamentals of the surface chemistry could provide hints on how one can tune the M to Ru ratio by precursor selection.

## Conclusions

In summary, we have reported a low temperature (&lt;125 °C) ALD route to amorphous metal ruthenates based on the use of RuO4 as the co-reactant, here exemplified for aluminium ruthenate and platinum ruthenate. Both the TMA/RuO4 and MeCpPtMe3/RuO4 process exhibit self-limiting surface reactions and linear growth as a function of the number of ALD cycles. The observed saturated GPCs (0.86 nm per cycle and

0.52 nm per cycle for the aluminium and platinum ruthenate process, respectively) are relatively high compared to common values for binary ALD processes. We propose that RuO4 combusts the organic ligands of the adsorbed precursor molecules, while at the same time binding a substantial amount of RuO2 to the surface. The presence of hydrogen and carbon impurities (both ca. 9% in the aluminium ruthenate films) nevertheless indicates that some ligand fragments can be incorporated in the film. More in-depth studies on the contamination levels and reaction mechanism of TMA and MeCpPtMe3 with a RuO2 surface are planned to understand the origin of the particular GPC values and metal (Al, Pt) to Ru ratios obtained in these processes.
