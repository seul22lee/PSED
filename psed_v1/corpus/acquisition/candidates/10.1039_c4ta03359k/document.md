## Introduction

Proton conducting materials are important in energy related applications, such as electricity generation in proton conducting fuel cells (PCFC), H2 generation in steam electrolysers, and as membranes for H2 gas separation. The proton conducting lanthanum tungstate (LWO), previously known as La6WO12 (ref. 1 -3) and recently reformulated to La28 /C0 x W4+ x O54+ d v2 /C0 d (0.74 &lt; x &lt; 1.08), 4,5 is a promising candidate for application in both PCFCs and H2 permeable membranes. At intermediate temperatures, LWO has a high proton conductivity and negligible electronic conductivity below 750 /C14 C (3 /C2 10 /C0 3 S cm /C0 1 at 750 /C14 C). 3,6,7 At temperatures above 800 /C14 C, in a reducing or oxidizing atmosphere, the material exhibits mixed ionic and electronic conductivity. 3,7 A high H2 permeability of 0.14 mL min /C0 1 cm /C0 2 (ref. 8 and 9) has been measured in an asymmetric LWO membrane at 1000 /C14 C, con  rming the possibility for the application of LWO as a H2 permeable membrane. LWO has also shown high chemical stability in CO2-rich and sulphurcontaining atmospheres, 9 high mechanical stability 10 as well as a thermal expansion coe /uniFB03 cient of /C24 11 -12 /C2 10 /C0 6 K /C0 1 (ref. 11 -13), making it compatible with device manufacturing. For both fuel cell and hydrogen membrane applications, the proton-conducting electrolyte should be as thin as possible while still retaining its pin-hole free character. Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) is a thin  lm deposition technique that inherently creates uniform, pin-hole free  lms even on geometrically complex surfaces. It is therefore perfectly suited for deposition of membranes and electrolytes. The  lm thickness is highly controllable by ALD in the low nm range, opening up for designs where thin membranes can be used to realize high  uxes at lower temperatures. This is a major advantage, as it allows the application of materials which traditionally would be regarded too resistive. The application of thin  lm designs does, however, introduce challenges with respect to mechanical instability, possible faster degradation, and higher overall leak currents. Such e /uniFB00 ects have to be taken into consideration in designing new electrolytes. An alternative approach to realization of proton-conducting membranes is to take advantage of the inherent capability of the ALD method to coat evenly on 3D substrates while still retaining the same quality of the  lms. The ALD technique can therefore be implemented as a subsequent step to traditional techniques in order to cover any remaining pin-hole on an already established electrolyte or a H2-permeable membrane.

It may be challenging to obtain the target accurate stoichiometry during deposition of complex oxides by the ALD technique. Such materials are typically prepared by combining two or more binary oxide processes sequentially. 15 The amount of

material deposited by each binary ALD cycle is process dependent and will o  en be a /uniFB00 ected by the surface chemistry of the preceding cycle. In the current work, we combine processes for deposition of the binary oxides, La2O3 and WO3, to obtain  lms within the La -W -O system. Deposition of La2O3 is previously well-known and is summarized in a recent comprehensive review 16 . There are, comparably, limited reports on deposition of WO3 by ALD. 17 -19 We currently expand and report on novel attempts and routes for deposition of WO3.

The main aim of the current work is to demonstrate the feasibility of using ALD for deposition of proton conducting oxide materials within the relevant La : W molar range of 5.3 -5.7 : 1 (ref. 5). The second target is to reliably measure the protonic conductivity by impedance spectroscopy, a task which frequently encounters major challenges, mostly due to formation of cracks or delamination in either  lms or contacts.

## Experimental

All  lms were deposited using an F120-SAT research -type ALD reactor (ASM Microchemistry) using a combination of di /uniFB00 erent processes for formation of tungsten and lanthanum oxides. The lanthanum oxide process was based on La(thd)3 and ozone, according to the procedure described by Nieminen et al. 20 La(thd)3 was sublimed inside the reactor at 180 /C14 C and introduced into the reaction chamber using inert gas valves. Ozone was produced by using a BMT 803N from OSTI inc, with oxygen from an ATF Oxygen Concentrator Module from Sequal Technologies. Tungsten oxides were deposited using either a combination of WOCl4 and H2O or ( t BuN)2(Me2N)2W (herea  er termed BMW) and H2O. H2O was evaporated from an external container at room temperature. WOCl4 was sublimed inside the reactor at 100 -180 /C14 C using inert gas valves, while the BMW precursor was delivered from an externally heated bubbler using a pressure cycling procedure consisting of  lling the 50 ml bubbler with 0.5 bar of inert gas and delivering this into the reaction chamber. The precursor temperature was subjected to optimization and 60 /C14 C was applied for the remaining experiments. The inert gas for the reactor was produced using a Schmidlin Sirocco 5 N2 generator (&lt;99.999%) at a total rate of 500 sccm resulting in an overall reactor pressure of approximately 4 mbar. The  lms were deposited on Si (100), Mg (100) and Pd. The substrates were cleaned using ethanol and pressurized air and exposed in situ to pulses of O3 prior to the deposition.

The cation atomic composition (cat%) of the  lms deposited on Si substrates was determined by X-ray  uorescence (XRF) using a Philips PW2400 XRF and analyzed by the Uniquant program. A J.E Woolham Alpha-SE spectroscopic ellipsometer was used to measure the thickness of the  lms by modelling the experimental data to a Cauchy function. X-ray di /uniFB00 ractograms (XRDs) were measured using a Bruker AXS D8 Discover powder di /uniFB00 ractometer equipped with a Ge(111) monochromator providing CuK a 1 radiation and recorded using a LynxEye detector. Scanning electron microscopy (Nova NanoSEM 650 from FEI) was used to obtain images of the  lms. In situ quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) measurements were conducted using home-made holders and recorded using a Maxtek TM400 unit.

Electrical measurements were conducted using two di /uniFB00 erent con  gurations. For the in-plane measurements, an insulating MgOsubstrate (1 /C2 1 cm 2 ) with the LWO  lm on top was coated with two stripes of /C24 500 nm of Pt separated by 0.5 mm by sputtering, using a Cressington 308R coating system. For the cross-plane measurements, an electron conducting and H2 permeable dense Pd substrate (20 mm diameter, 0.3 mm thickness) was used. In this case, several contact points of 3 mm and /C24 500 nm thick Pt were coated by sputtering as a counter electrode, and the electrodes that did not short-circuit a  er annealing were measured. In all cases, Pt grid and wires were attached to either electrode in a 2-point-4-wire setup. Impedance spectra were collected at di /uniFB00 erent temperatures and atmospheres using an impedance analyzer (Novocontrol Alpha A, Novocontrol Technologies) in the 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz frequency range with an AC perturbation of 50 mV. The thicknesses of the LWO  lms were approximately 1.2 m m on Pd and 0.9 m m on MgO. The  lm and electrode were annealed at 800 /C14 C for 30 min prior to the analysis to increase the  lm crystallinity and electrode adherence.

## Results and discussion

As mentioned in the Introduction, the aim of the current project is to develop thin  lms in the La -W -O series by combining processes of deposition of binary lanthanum and tungsten oxides. The process of deposition of lanthanum oxide was adopted from the work of Nieminen et al. 20 and is described in more detail in the section of La -W -O deposition.

## WO3 deposition

The prior experience in deposition of tungsten oxides is relatively limited. We chose to investigate the possible application of WOCl4 and BMW due to their availability and possible simplicity. WOCl4 should on  rst impression be a simple type of precursor, however, with potential inclusion of chlorine during deposition. While chlorine incorporation is not a challenge with BMW,its current cost is considerably higher. The application of WCl6 + H2O as a precursor combination was not considered practical due to likely formation of volatile WOCl4, in a similar manner to that reported for the application of NbCl5 with H2O. 21 In the same work by Knapas et al. , however, they showed that NbOCl3 can be applied to the formation of  lms of Nb2O5. Previous work on deposition of WO3  lms by WF6 (ref. 17) has revealed limited  lm formation due to  lm etching in the same manner as mentioned above, whereas in situ generated WO y F x proved more successful and a much higher growth rate was obtained. It is therefore likely that the same will apply to the chloride analogue. Previous studies have also shown successful deposition of WO3 using the BMW precursor, 18 which we have expanded upon in terms of growth temperatures, in addition to further investigation into the ALD/CVD process.

## WOCl4

The attempts to deposit WO3 using WOCl4 as a cation precursor yielded no growth on Si substrates at  rst, despite variations of the precursor temperature (100 -180 /C14 C), substrate temperature (150 -300 /C14 C), reactant (H2O, O3, H2O + O3), and pulsing time (3 -25 s). However, signs of deposition were observed when depositing on  lms of La2O3 or Al2O3 on Si substrates.

An in situ QCManalysis, Fig. 1 and 2, was conducted at 275 /C14 C to obtain some information on the role of the substrate during deposition, speci  cally on La2O3. A thin layer of WO3 was deposited on the QCM crystal as a nucleation layer using 20 cycles of the BMW + H2O process described later. The thickness of WO3 was estimated from the WO3 growth rates to be less than a monolayer (1.6 ˚ A), leaving the Au surface of the QCM only partially covered. No  lm was observed when WOCl4 + H2O were pulsed directly on this surface (Fig. 1a and 2e). Interestingly, while no growth was seen when a long wait time was added between deposition of the nucleation layer and the WOCl4 + H2O process, some  lm formation was detected when pulsing WOCl4 + H2O directly a  er the BMW + H2O process (Fig. 2c). These results were reproducible. Therea  er, a thin layer of La2O3 (13 ˚ A) was deposited using 40 cycles of the La(thd)3 + O3 process before the WO3 nucleation layer and the WOCl4 + H2O process was repeated. This resulted in formation of a limited amount of material. The growth rate a  er an initiation period of a few cycles was reasonably high but non-linear (Fig. 1), before tapering o /uniFB00 to a very low but linear growth rate within the range studied (Fig. 2d). While the growth did not cease during the limited number of cycles applied in the QCM measurements, prolonging the exposures of the WOCl4 + H2O pair on La2O3 from 1000 to 3000 cycles did not reveal any additional  lm formation. A similar experiment on Al2O3, where the number of cycles on the Al2O3  lm increased from 1000 to 2500, gave the same negative result. The  lm formation was limited to less than two nanometers. ## La -W -O deposition

The La -W -O system was investigated by combining the deposition processes for La2O3 and WO3. The number of La cycles [La(thd)3 + O3] between each W cycle [BMW + H2O] was varied from 10 (91 pulse%) to 17 (94.5 pulse%). The pulse and purge parameters used for this investigation were higher than those su /uniFB03 cient for saturation and ensured ALD growth, and were set at 3/6/3/10 s for La2O3 and 5/5/3/5 s for WO3. In particular, the BMW precursor appears to react strongly with any remaining ozone from the La-cycle, and an extra purge of 60 s was used before introducing the BMW precursor.

The average growth rate increases with La-content as seen in Fig. 5. This is expected due to the higher growth rate of La2O3 as compared to WO3. The observed growth rates were, however, lower than a direct combination of the growth rates of the binary oxides. As discussed previously, a surface enhanced growth of WO3 is seen when deposited on La2O3. Therefore, these results suggest that the growth of La2O3 is surface inhibited when deposited on WO3.

## La28 /C0 x W4+ x O54+ d v 2 /C0 d deposition for electrical measurements

Pulsing of 91.4% La(thd)3 cycles (pulsing relationship 10.67 : 1) gave  lms close to the LWO54 composition (5.4 : 1 ratio for La : W molar ratio). This pulsing ratio was achieved by alternating the macrocycles 10 : 1 and 11 : 1 in a 1 : 2 ratio.

A 0.9 m mthick LWO  lm was deposited on MgO (100) for inplane measurement and a 1.2 m m thick  lm was deposited on polycrystalline Pd for cross-plane measurements. This thickness of the  lms allows the resistance through the  lm to be reliably measured. For Pd substrates, two di /uniFB00 erent methods were used to obtain thick LWO  lms, termed method 1 (M1) and method 2 (M2). M1 was the same as that for MgO substrates, using continuous ALD deposition, only stopping the reactor to re  ll the La(thd)3 precursor every 22 h. With M2, 40 nm LWO  lms were initially deposited on the Pd substrates by ALD and heat treated at 850 /C14 C for 30 min in air. These were subsequently used as substrates for further deposition of approximately 1.16 m mof continuous LWO deposition as in the  rst method. The second method was established to attempt avoiding cracking and short-circuiting of the  lm during measurements.

The SEM images in Fig. 7 of the as-deposited crystalline LWO  lms show smooth and crack-free  lms, appearing to be pinhole-free (Fig. 7a and b). ## Conclusions

Thin  lms of lanthanum tungstate (LWO) were produced using atomic layer deposition by combining the binary deposition processes of La2O3 and WO3. The precursor La(thd)3 in combination with ozone was used for deposition of La2O3. Deposition of WO3 using the cation precursors, WOCl4 and ( t BuN)2(Me2N)2W(VI) (BMW), was investigated, where WOCl4 was found to provide growth on La2O3 and Al2O3, but not on itself, and was therefore not useful for LWO deposition. The cation precursor BMW in combination with water gave ALDtype growth between 275 and 325 /C14 C, and was therefore utilized in the LWO deposition. A series of La -W -O  lms were deposited, for exploring the change in achieved composition with pulsing percentages. Films with La-pulsing percentage between 91.3 and 92.4% appeared to fall within the composition range for the proton conducting LWO phase, or a pulsed relationship of 10.5 : 1 and 12 : 1 between La(thd)3 and BMW.

1.2 and 0.9 m m thick crystalline  lms with a La-pulsing percentage of 91.4% were deposited on Pd and MgO, respectively. These were con  rmed as the proton-conducting phase of LWO by XRD. The  lms were pin-hole free as-deposited. The LWO  lm on Pd cracked during annealing, causing short-circuiting in all cells for electrical characterization. An alternative deposition procedure was therefore devised, where a thin layer of LWO  lm (40 nm) was deposited on the substrate and subsequently annealed. A 1.16 m m LWO  lm was therea  er deposited on top of the annealed LWO  lm. This procedure was successful in avoiding short-circuiting of LWO  lms during electrical measurements, and the electrical properties were reproducibly measured both in cross-plane and in-plane con  gurations.

The LWO  lms behave mainly as an oxygen ion conductor at high temperatures, but mainly as a proton conductor at lower temperatures and under wet conditions, with some contributions from p-type conductivity under oxidizing conditions, and a more signi  cant contribution from n-type electronic conductivity in highly reducing atmospheres. The in-plane and cross-plane measurements for  lms on MgO and Pd, respectively, are consistent. The magnitude and the temperature dependence of the conductivity in LWO  lms grown by ALD di /uniFB00 er from the bulk values. This may be attributed to internal doping e /uniFB00 ects.
