## Semiconductor-metal transition in thin VO2 films grown by ozone based atomic layer deposition

( Received 8 March 2011; accepted 25 March 2011; published online 19 April 2011 )

Thin films of vanadium dioxide ( VO2 ) have been grown by a low temperature atomic layer deposition process at 150 °C using tetrakis [ ethylmethylamino ] vanadium as a vanadium source and ozone as reactant gas. Films deposited on SiO 2 were amorphous, but during a thermal treatment at 450 °C tetragonal VO2 ( R ) was formed. During in situ x-ray diffraction measurements, the semiconductor-metal transition was observed as a reversible transition between VO2 ( M1 ) and VO2 ( R ) near 67 °C. Correlated with this phase change, a reversible change in resistivity was observed of more than two orders of magnitude for a film of 42 nm thickness. © 2011 American Institute of Physics . [ doi:10.1063/1.3579195 ]

In the large group of vanadium oxides, VO 2 has the interesting feature that a large reversible change in resistivity occurs near a temperature of 68 °C, 1 which is referred to as a metal-insulator transition or semiconductor-metal transition ( SMT ) . The transition is known to be accompanied with a transformation in crystallographic structure, from monoclinic VO 2 ( M1 ) below the transition temperature to tetragonal VO2 ( R ) above. 2 For single crystals the resistivity change can be as high as five orders of magnitude and occurs in a very narrow temperature range of only a few degrees Celsius. 3 This property makes VO2 an interesting candidate for applications in resistive memories and switches in microelectronics. In that case one is interested in inducing the resistive switch electrically rather than thermally and several groups succeeded already in making such test devices. 4,5

A lot of results are reported in literature on thin film growth of VO2, typically using pulsed laser deposition, 6 reactive sputtering, 7,8 or chemical vapor deposition 9-11 as deposition techniques. For applications in microelectronic devices, however, atomic layer deposition ( ALD ) is gaining importance for depositing oxide films, due to aggressive scaling of device dimensions and increasing integration complexity, requiring in many cases conformal depositions in three-dimensional structures. The key feature for this technique is the self-limiting character of the surface reactions, making layer by layer growth possible. 12,13 The main advantages are the excellent control of thickness and stoichiometry and a very good uniformity and conformality on complex structures. Also the typical deposition temperatures are rather low compared to other deposition techniques. However, ALD processes for vanadium oxides mostly result in V2O5 growth. 14

In this work, it is shown that amorphous VO 2 films can be deposited with an ALD process at only 150 °C. During an additional thermal treatment at 450 °C in inert gas VO 2 ( R ) is observed as the first and only crystalline phase, which on cooling down transforms to VO2 ( M1 ) . A SMT is observed

Thin vanadium oxide films were grown in an experimental low-pressure ALD reactor 15,16 on Si substrates with 100 nm thermally grown SiO2. Tetrakis [ ethylmethylamino ] vanadium { V ( NEtMe ) 4 } , supplied by Air Liquide and denoted here as TEMAV, was used as metal-organic vanadium precursor, which has an oxidation state of 4+ for the vanadium, in contrast to other vanadium precursors like VO [ O ( C3H7 ) i ] 3 where it is 5+. This could possibly facilitate the formation of VO2 instead of V 2 O 5 , since 4+ is also the oxidation state of vanadium in VO2. As reactant gasses, both H 2 O ( thermal ) , O2 ( plasma enhanced ) and O3 were tested. Although it seemed possible to obtain similar results with all three reactants, the process with O3 behaved as the most reliable. Therefore, only results of the ozone based ALD process will be discussed in detail in this letter. The ozone was produced from a pure flow of O 2 with an OzoneLab™ OL100, resulting in an O 3 concentration of 175 𝛍 g / ml. The base pressure in the reactor was 10 -6 mbar, whereas the partial pressures for TEMAV and ozone were 10 -4 mbar and 5 × 10 -1 mbar, respectively. Ar was used as a carrier gas for the TEMAV. Since the main characteristic of ALD is the self-limiting nature of the surface reactions, it is important to verify in which temperature range this behavior is observed. Therefore, a process was performed at several substrate temperatures, consisting of 200 times 2 s exposure with TEMAV while no reactant gas was used. At 200 °C, a film was grown with a growth rate of 0.3 Å/s of TEMAV exposure as derived from an x-ray reflectivity ( XRR ) measurement, corresponding to thermal decomposition of the precursor. While no clearly detectable layer was observed with XRR when the temperature was lowered to 175 °C, x-ray fluorescence ( XRF ) revealed that there was still some vanadium deposited. The XRF signal ratio V/Si was ten times lower than the deposition at 200 °C. Although very low, this signal was again lowered five times at 150 °C substrate temperature. More evidence for the assumption of VO2 ( R ) is provided by the observation that the diffracted peak position shifted between 27.85° and 27.65° when the sample temperature was varied between 30 and 100 °C [ Fig. 5 ( a )] . This corresponded with the reversible transformation between VO2 ( M1 ) ( JCPDS 44-0252 ) at low temperature and VO2 ( R ) at high temperature. In addition, Fig. 3 also shows the XRD pattern for the annealed film, measured at room temperature. The transition temperature was approximately 73 °C during heating and 61 °C during cooling. Thus, the mean transition temperature was 67 °C and the hysteresis 12 °C.

In the same heating stage as used for the in situ XRD measurements 2 point resistivity measurements were performed with a Keithley 2000 Multimeter in order to correlate the crystallographic phase transformation with a change in resistivity. Since the measured resistivities were in the range of kilo-ohms or higher, the contact resistance was negligible and the use of a 2 probe setup was justified. In addition, 4 point resistivity measurements were performed at room temperature in order to obtain absolute resistivity values. Figure 5 ( b ) shows the resistivity as a function of temperature for the 42 nm film crystallized by annealing three times 10 min at 450 °C in He. A SMT is clearly observed with a transition temperature a few degrees Celsius lower than the crystallographic phase transformation and a resistivity ratio of approximately 200 in the range 30-100 °C. Transition temperatures were derived by fitting a Gaussian curve to d [ -log ( R )] / d T for both heating and cooling [ Fig. 5 ( c )] . This resulted in a mean transition temperature of 63 °C, which is 4 °C lower than derived from in situ XRD, and a hysteresis of 12 °C.

In conclusion, ozone based ALD was used to deposit thin films of vanadium oxide, with TEMAV as a vanadium precursor. XPS revealed that the stoichiometry was VO2. Since all films were amorphous, isothermal anneals were applied in an inert atmosphere, resulting in tetragonal VO 2 ( R ) . In situ XRD measurements also showed a reversible transformation between VO2 ( M1 ) and VO2 ( R ) near 67 °C with a hysteresis of approximately 12 °C. This crystallographic phase transformation was correlated with a SMT as observed during resistivity measurements. The mean transition temperature in this case has been derived as 63 °C, with a hysteresis of 12 °C. The resistivity dropped by a factor of 200 in the range 30-100 °C for a 42 nm film.
